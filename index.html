1. Introduction, Scope, Limitations & Types of Systems

Foundations
Thermodynamics: The study of the flow of heat or any other form of energy into or out of a system during a physical or chemical change. By measuring changes in Temperature (T), Pressure (P), Volume (V), and Concentration from initial to final state, we find changes in energy, heat, and work.

Three Empirical Laws

  • Based on experimental results (1st, 2nd, and 3rd laws).
  • Completely independent of atomic or molecular structure theories.

Scope (What it DOES)

  • Derives key laws: van't Hoff law (lowering of vapor pressure), Phase Rule, Distribution Law.
  • Predicts feasibility (can a change happen under given T, P, conc.?).
  • Predicts extent (how far a change proceeds until equilibrium).

Limitations (What it IGNORES)

  • Macroscopic only: Applies to bulk matter, NOT single atoms/molecules; ignores internal atomic structure.
  • No time factor: Tells nothing about reaction speed/rate—cares only about initial & final states.

System, Boundary & Surroundings

  • System: The specific part of the universe under study (e.g., 0.2 kg water in a beaker, or 1 mole O2 in a cylinder with a piston).
  • Surroundings: Everything else in the universe outside the system (beaker, air, piston).
  • Boundary: The real or imaginary surface separating system from surroundings.

Homogeneous vs Heterogeneous Systems

  • Phase: A homogeneous, physically distinct, mechanically separable part of a system.
  • Homogeneous System: Uniform throughout; 1 phase only (e.g., pure solid/liquid/gas, gas mixture, true salt solution).
  • Heterogeneous System: Not uniform; 2 or more phases (e.g., ice + liquid water, ice + vapor).

1. Isolated System

Boundary is sealed & insulated. Transfers neither matter nor energy.
Ex: Boiling water in a thermos flask; water + vapor in a closed insulated vessel.

2. Closed System

Boundary is sealed, not insulated. Transfers energy (heat, work, radiation) but NO matter.
Ex: Hot water in a sealed tube; gas in a piston-cylinder.

3. Open System

Boundary is open & un-insulated. Transfers both matter & energy.
Ex: Hot water in an open beaker; Zinc + dil. HCl reacting in a beaker (H2 escapes).

4. Adiabatic System

A system in which no thermal energy (heat) passes into or out of the system (may still exchange work).

2. Intensive & Extensive Properties, State & Equilibrium

Properties & States

Intensive vs Extensive Properties

Easy Trick: Imagine splitting a glass of water in half. Whatever stays the same is Intensive; whatever gets halved is Extensive.

Intensive (Quantity-Independent)Extensive (Quantity-Dependent)
Not additive. Same in any drop of the system.
Examples: Temperature, Pressure, Density, Concentration, Surface tension, Refractive index, Viscosity, Boiling point, Freezing point.		 Additive. Doubles if mass doubles.
Examples: Mass, Volume, Number of moles, Internal energy (E), Enthalpy (H), Entropy (S), Gibbs free energy (G).

State of a System & Equation of State

  • State: Defined when all macroscopic properties (P, V, T, m, composition) are fixed.
  • State Variables / State Functions: Properties whose change depends only on initial & final states, NOT on the path taken (e.g., P, V, T, E, H, S).
  • Path Functions: Depend on how the process is performed (e.g., Heat q, Work w, Heat Capacity C).
  • Equation of State: Connects P, V, T (for 1 mol ideal gas: PV = RT). Specifying Independent variables (e.g., P & T) automatically fixes the Dependent variable (V).

Equilibrium vs Non-Equilibrium States

  • Thermodynamic Equilibrium: State variables are uniform and constant throughout the system (e.g., stationary frictionless piston). Thermodynamics deals only with equilibrium states.
  • Non-Equilibrium State: State variables vary from place to place (e.g., gas compressed rapidly—gas near the piston is hotter & denser than at the far end).

3 Criteria for Equilibrium

  1. Thermal Equilibrium: Temperature is uniform throughout & equals surroundings.
  2. Mechanical Equilibrium: Mechanical properties/pressure uniform; no part does mechanical work on another.
  3. Chemical Equilibrium: Chemical composition is uniform; no net chemical reaction occurs. (In heterogeneous systems, variables remain constant in each phase.)

3. Thermodynamic Processes & Reversible vs Irreversible

Processes

1. Isothermal Process

Temperature stays constant (often in a thermostat bath).
dT = 0

2. Adiabatic Process

No heat flows in or out (insulated vessel/thermos).
dq = 0

3. Isobaric Process

Pressure stays constant (e.g., boiling in open air).
dP = 0

4. Isochoric Process

Volume stays constant (sealed rigid container).
dV = 0

5. Cyclic Process

System passes through several processes and returns to its exact initial state.
dE = 0   dH = 0 (all state functions have net change = 0)

Reversible vs Irreversible Processes

Imagine a gas in a cylinder under a weightless, frictionless piston: decreasing external pressure by tiny steps dP gives slow reversible expansion (always in equilibrium); dropping pressure suddenly in one step gives rapid irreversible expansion.

Reversible ProcessIrreversible Process
1. Proceeds in infinite infinitesimally small steps; takes infinite time.1. Proceeds in a single/few steps; takes finite time.
2. Imaginary / ideal (assumes frictionless, weightless piston).2. Real and can actually be performed in lab/nature.
3. System is in equilibrium at all stages.3. Equilibrium exists only at initial and final states.
4. Direction can be reversed at any point by an infinitesimal change; all changes reverse.4. Changes do not return to initial state by themselves.
5. Extremely (infinitely) slow.5. Proceeds at a measurable speed.
6. Work done in expansion is maximum (greater).6. Work done in expansion is smaller than reversible work.

4. Nature of Heat, Work & Pressure-Volume (PV) Work

Heat & Work

Work & Heat Units

  • Work: Force × distance.
  • CGS unit: erg (1 dyne moved 1 cm).
  • SI unit (both Work & Heat): Joule (J).
  • 1 J = 107 ergs | 1 kJ = 1000 J
  • Calorie: Heat to raise 1 g water by 1°C (~15°C).
  • 1 cal = 4.184 J | 1 J = 0.2390 cal

Sign Conventions (Textbook Rule)

  • Heat absorbed by system: +q
  • Heat given off by system: −q
  • Work done on system (compression): +w
  • Work done by system (expansion): −w
Sign Tip for Exams: This book defines work done by system as negative (w = −PextΔV), yet writes First Law as ΔE = q − w (substituting positive magnitude w = PΔV). Be careful: check whether a numerical asks for work with sign (expansion = −) or magnitude of work done by gas (+).

Pressure–Volume (PV) Work

When a gas expands against a constant external pressure Pext (NOT internal pressure!):

w = − Pext × ΔV = − Pext(V2 − V1)
  • Expansion (V2 > V1): work is done by gas (w is −ve).
  • Compression (V2 < V1): work is done on gas (w is +ve).
  • Work is a path function, NOT a state function.

Isothermal Expansion Work of an Ideal Gas

A. Reversible Expansion (Pext = Pgas − dP ≈ P = nRT/V):

wrev = − ∫V₁V₂ P dV = − nRT lnV2V1 = − 2.303 nRT logV2V1 = − 2.303 nRT logP1P2

B. Irreversible Expansion (sudden drop to constant P2):

wirrev = − P2(V2 − V1)

Compression has the exact same expressions with opposite sign (+). Note: Units of R determine units of w, and T must be in Kelvin (K).

Why Reversible Expansion Gives Maximum Work

Work depends on Pext. Larger Pext = more work done by the gas. However, Pext cannot exceed Pgas (otherwise compression occurs!). The largest possible Pext during expansion is Pext ≈ Pgas — which is the exact condition for a reversible expansion.

5. Internal Energy & First Law of Thermodynamics

First Law

Internal Energy (E or U)

  • Definition: Total of all possible kinds of energy within a system (translational, rotational, vibrational kinetic energies + kinetic & potential energy of nuclei and electrons).
  • State Function & Extensive Property: Depends only on state and mass; independent of how the state was reached.
  • Absolute value cannot be measured—we measure only the change: ΔE = Efinal − Einitial
  • Units: Joule (J) [SI], Calorie (1 cal = 4.184 J).

First Law of Thermodynamics

Application of the Law of Conservation of Energy:

  • Total energy of an isolated system (or system + surroundings) remains constant.
  • Energy cannot be created or destroyed; if one form disappears, an equivalent amount of another appears.
  • Impossible to build a perpetual motion machine of the 1st kind (producing work without spending energy).
ΔE = q − w   or   ΔE = q − PΔV   (for PV work)

Case 1: Cyclic Process

Returns to start ⇒ ΔE = 0
Therefore: q = w

Case 2: Isochoric (V const)

No expansion (w = 0)
Therefore: ΔE = qv

Case 3: Adiabatic (q = 0)

No heat exchange
Therefore: ΔE = −w

Case 4: Isobaric (P const)

Constant pressure
ΔE = q − PΔV

6. Enthalpy (H) & Molar Heat Capacities (Cp, Cv)

Enthalpy & Heat Capacity

Enthalpy (H) — Total Heat Content at Constant P

H = E + PV
  • Since E, P, V are state functions, Enthalpy H is a state function (and extensive property).
  • At constant pressure: ΔH = ΔE + PΔV = qp (heat absorbed/evolved at constant pressure).
  • Endothermic: H2 > H1 ⇒ ΔH is +ve (heat absorbed).
  • Exothermic: H1 > H2 ⇒ ΔH is −ve (heat released).
  • Units: kJ (SI) or kcal.

Relation Between ΔH and ΔE

Solids/liquids have negligible volume change compared to gases. If gaseous moles change from n1 (reactants) to n2 (products) for ideal gases:

PV1 = n1RT,   PV2 = n2RT ⇒ PΔV = (n2 − n1)RT = ΔngRT

ΔH = ΔE + ΔngRT

When does ΔH = ΔE? When Δng = 0 (e.g., H2 + I2 ⇌ 2HI), or when no gases are involved, or at constant volume (ΔV = 0).

Heat Capacity & Molar Heat Capacity

  • Heat capacity (c): Heat absorbed by unit mass to raise T by 1 K: c = q / [m(T2 − T1)]
  • Molar Heat Capacity (C): Heat required to raise temperature of 1 mole by 1 K: C = dq / dT
  • Units: J K−1 mol−1 (SI) or cal K−1 mol−1.
  • Note: Heat and heat capacity are NOT state functions, so we specify constant V or constant P.
  • At Constant Volume (Cv): Cv = (∂E / ∂T)V (rate of change of E with T at constant V).
  • At Constant Pressure (Cp): Cp = (∂H / ∂T)P (rate of change of H with T at constant P).

Why Cp > Cv & Relation Cp − Cv = R

At constant V, all absorbed heat raises temperature (ΔE). At constant P, part of the heat raises temperature and the rest does PV expansion work—so more heat is needed per 1 K rise ⇒ Cp > Cv.

Derivation (1 mol ideal gas):
H = E + PV = E + RT → Differentiate w.r.t. T:
dH/dT = dE/dT + R ⇒ Cp = Cv + R

Cp − Cv = R (= 8.314 J K−1 mol−1 or 1.987 cal K−1 mol−1)

For n moles of an ideal gas:   ΔE = n Cv (T2 − T1)  |  ΔH = n Cp (T2 − T1)

7. Joule-Thomson Effect (Adiabatic Throttling)

Real Gases & Cooling

Concept & Proof of Constant Enthalpy

Joule-Thomson Effect: Lowering of temperature when a compressed gas expands adiabatically through a porous plug from high pressure (P1) to low pressure (P2).

  • Work done on gas at piston A = P1V1; work done by gas at piston B = P2V2.
  • Net work done by gas: w = P2V2 − P1V1
  • Since adiabatic (q = 0): E2 − E1 = −(P2V2 − P1V1)
  • Rearranging: E2 + P2V2 = E1 + P1V1 ⇒ H2 = H1 (ΔH = 0)
Joule-Thomson expansion is an isenthalpic process (takes place at constant enthalpy).

Joule-Thomson Coefficient (μ) & Inversion Temp

Temperature change per atm pressure drop at constant enthalpy:

μJT = ∂T∂PH
  • μ > 0 (+ve): Gas cools on expansion (most gases at room T; used in gas liquefaction).
  • μ < 0 (−ve): Gas warms on expansion (e.g., H2 and He at room temp).
  • μ = 0: Ideal gas (no intermolecular forces; zero temp change).
  • Inversion Temperature (Ti): Temperature where μ changes sign. Below Ti, H2 (~193 K / −80°C) and He (~40 K) also cool on expansion.
  • Explanation: Since H = E + PV = constant, if PV increases during expansion, internal energy E decreases (cooling). For H2/He at room T, PV decreases, so E increases (warming).

8. Adiabatic Expansion of an Ideal Gas & Adiabatic Work

Adiabatic Relations

Three Adiabatic Gas Equations (γ = Cp / Cv)

Since q = 0, expansion work comes from internal energy: dE = −P dV ⇒ Cv dT = −(RT/V) dV. Integrating and using R/Cv = (Cp − Cv)/Cv = γ − 1 gives:

1) T1V1γ−1 = T2V2γ−1   (TVγ−1 = const)
2) P1V1γ = P2V2γ     (PVγ = const)
3) T1γP11−γ = T2γP21−γ  or  T2T1 = (P2P1)(γ−1)/γ

For a monoatomic ideal gas, γ = 5/3 = 1.67; for diatomic gas, γ ≈ 1.4.

Work Done in Adiabatic Reversible Expansion

Since q = 0, work done by the gas equals the decrease in internal energy (−ΔE):

wmax = nR(T1 − T2)γ − 1 = P1V1 − P2V2γ − 1 = n Cv (T1 − T2)
  • If T2 < T1 (expansion/cooling): wmax is +ve (work done by the gas).
  • If T2 > T1 (compression/heating): wmax is −ve (work done on the gas).

Isothermal vs Adiabatic Expansion

  • Isothermal (PV = const): Heat is absorbed from surroundings to replace energy lost as work ⇒ Temperature stays constant.
  • Adiabatic (PVγ = const): No heat enters; work is done at the expense of internal energy ⇒ Temperature drops (T must change!).
  • Because γ > 1 and pressure drops faster due to cooling, the adiabatic P-V curve is steeper than the isothermal curve.

10. Key Solved Numericals (Click to Expand)

Exam Practice
Prob 1 (PV Work): Gas expands from 0.001 m³ to 0.002 m³ against 1013250 Pa. Find work in J & cal.
w = −Pext(V2 − V1) = −1013250 Pa × (0.002 − 0.001) m³ = −1013.25 J.
In calories: −1013.25 / 4.184 = −242.17 cal.
Prob 2 & 3 (Isothermal Reversible Work): 1 mol ideal gas at 298 K expands (a) 0.01 → 0.02 m³, (b) 5 atm → 1 atm.
(a) Volume form: |w| = 2.303 × 1 × 8.314 × 298 × log(0.02 / 0.01) = 5705.8 × 0.3010 = 1717.46 J (410.48 cal; −1717.46 J with expansion sign).
(b) Pressure form: |w| = 2.303 × 1 × 8.314 × 298 × log(5 / 1) = 5705.8 × 0.6990 = 3988.2 J (−3988.2 J with expansion sign).
Prob 5 (Vaporization & 1st Law): 0.001 kg water at 373 K boils to steam (V₂ = 0.001671 m³) at 1 atm. ΔHvap = 2260980 J/kg. Find ΔE.
Heat absorbed: q = 0.001 kg × 2260980 J/kg = 2260.98 J.
Volume of liquid water ≈ 10−6 m³ (negligible vs 0.001671 m³).
Work done by system: w = PΔV = 101325 × (0.001671 − 0.000001) = 169.21 J.
ΔE = q − w = 2260.98 − 169.21 = 2091.77 J.
Prob 6 (First Law): Gas expands from 0.005 m³ to 0.01 m³ against 101325 Pa while absorbing 400 J heat. Find ΔE.
Work done by gas: w = PΔV = 101325 × (0.01 − 0.005) = 506.625 J.
Heat absorbed: q = +400 J.
ΔE = q − w = 400 − 506.625 = −106.625 J.
Prob 8 & 13 (ΔH vs ΔE): (a) H₂F₂(g) → H₂(g) + F₂(g), ΔE = −59.45 kJ at 298 K. Find ΔH. (b) Combustion of 1 mol C₆H₆(l) gives ΔH = −3269.81 kJ at 298 K. Find ΔE.
(a) Δng = 2 − 1 = +1. Convert R to kJ: RT = 8.314 × 10−3 × 298 = 2.478 kJ/mol.
ΔH = ΔE + ΔngRT = −59.45 + 2.48 = −56.97 kJ/mol.
(b) C6H6(l) + 7.5 O2(g) → 6 CO2(g) + 3 H2O(l). Only count gases: Δng = 6 − 7.5 = −1.5.
ΔE = ΔH − ΔngRT = −3269.807 kJ − (−1.5 × 2.478 kJ) = −3266.09 kJ.
Prob 16 (Heating O₂): Calculate ΔE and ΔH on heating 0.064 kg O₂ from 273 K to 373 K. (Cv = 20.93, Cp = 29.31 J/mol·K)
Moles of O2 (molar mass = 0.032 kg/mol): n = 0.064 / 0.032 = 2 mol; ΔT = 373 − 273 = 100 K.
ΔE = n Cv ΔT = 2 × 20.93 × 100 = 4186 J.
ΔH = n Cp ΔT = 2 × 29.31 × 100 = 5862 J.
Prob 12 (Adiabatic Work): 2 moles of monoatomic ideal gas (γ = 5/3) at 273.2 K expand adiabatically & reversibly from 2026500 Pa to 202650 Pa. Find T₂ and w.
Using T2/T1 = (P2/P1)(γ−1)/γ where (γ−1)/γ = (2/3)/(5/3) = 2/5 = 0.4:
T2 = 273.2 × (202650 / 2026500)0.4 = 273.2 × (0.1)0.4 = 273.2 × 0.3981 = 108.76 K.
w = nR(T1 − T2)/(γ − 1) = [2 × 8.314 × (273.2 − 108.76)] / (2/3) = 4101.8 J (done by gas).
