First Law of Thermodynamics — Basic Concepts

A complete, structured study guide covering thermodynamic systems, properties, processes, PV work, internal energy, enthalpy, molar heat capacities, the Joule–Thomson effect, and adiabatic expansions—with derivations, visual diagrams, and solved problems.

1

Introduction, Scope & Limitations

Definition: Thermodynamics Thermodynamics is the study of the flow of heat or any other form of energy into or out of a system as it undergoes a physical or chemical transformation.

Energy flow is evaluated by measuring changes in macroscopic properties—such as temperature, pressure, volume, and concentration—between the initial and final states of a system.

The Three Empirical Laws

Thermodynamics rests on three broad generalizations derived purely from experimental observations: the First, Second, and Third Laws of Thermodynamics. They are completely independent of any theory of atomic or molecular structure.

Scope of Thermodynamics
  • Derives fundamental laws: Including van’t Hoff’s law of lowering of vapor pressure, Phase Rule, and Distribution Law.
  • Predicts feasibility: Tells whether a physical or chemical change can occur under given temperature, pressure, and concentration.
  • Predicts extent of reaction: Determines how far a change proceeds before reaching equilibrium.
Limitations of Thermodynamics
  • Macroscopic only: Applies to bulk matter, not individual atoms or molecules; ignores internal atomic/molecular structure.
  • Ignores time & rate: Tells nothing about reaction speed—only concerned with initial and final states.

Thermodynamic Terms, Systems & Properties

System, Boundary, and Surroundings

  • System: The specific part of the universe under thermodynamic study (e.g., 0.200 kg of water in a beaker, or 1 mol of O2 in a cylinder with a piston).
  • Surroundings: The rest of the universe outside the system (e.g., the beaker and surrounding air, or the cylinder, piston, and external environment).
  • Boundary: The real or imaginary surface separating the system from the surroundings.

Homogeneous vs. Heterogeneous Systems

Homogeneous System Uniform throughout; consists of one phase only.
Examples: Pure solid/liquid/gas, mixture of gases, true solution of a solid in a liquid.
Heterogeneous System Not uniform throughout; consists of two or more phases.
Examples: Ice + water, ice + vapor (each forms a distinct phase).

Phase: A homogeneous, physically distinct, and mechanically separable portion of a system.

Types of Thermodynamic Systems

1. Isolated System Boundary is sealed & insulated. Transfers neither matter nor energy.
Ex: Boiling water in a thermos flask; 100 mL water + vapor in a closed insulated vessel.
2. Closed System Boundary is sealed, not insulated. Transfers energy (heat, work, radiation), but no matter.
Ex: Hot water in a sealed tube; gas in a cylinder fitted with a piston.
3. Open System Boundary is open & un-insulated. Transfers both matter and energy.
Ex: Hot water in an open beaker; Zn granules + dilute HCl in a beaker.
Figure: Comparison of Isolated, Closed, and Open thermodynamic systems.
Adiabatic Systems Systems in which no thermal energy (heat) passes into or out of the system are called adiabatic systems.

Intensive vs. Extensive Properties

Intensive Properties Do not depend on the quantity of matter present. They are non-additive (e.g., if a glass of water is at 293 K with 0.1 M NaCl, any single drop also has 293 K and 0.1 M concentration).
Extensive Properties Depend on the quantity of matter present. They are additive (doubling the mass of water doubles its volume, moles, and internal energy).
Intensive Properties (Independent of Mass) Extensive Properties (Dependent on Mass)
Temperature, Pressure, Density, Concentration Mass, Volume, Number of moles (n)
Boiling point, Freezing point Internal energy (E), Enthalpy (H)
Surface tension, Refractive index, Viscosity Entropy (S), Gibbs free energy (G)

State of a System & Thermodynamic Equilibrium

  • State of a System: A system is in a definite state when all its macroscopic properties are fixed.
  • State Variables (State Functions): Properties like P, V, T, mass, and composition whose changes depend only on the initial and final states, not on the path taken (e.g., heating water from 273.15 K to 298.15 K always gives ΔT = 25 K regardless of how it was heated).
  • Equation of State: Algebraic link between state variables, e.g., PV = RT (for 1 mol of an ideal gas). Specifying independent state variables (e.g., P and T) automatically fixes the dependent state variable (V).
Equilibrium State State variables have constant, uniform values throughout the system (e.g., gas in a cylinder with a stationary frictionless piston). Thermodynamics deals only with equilibrium states.
Non-Equilibrium State State variables vary across different parts of the system (e.g., gas compressed very rapidly—gas near the piston is hotter and denser than at the far end).
Three Criteria for Thermodynamic Equilibrium 1. Thermal equilibrium: Temperature is uniform throughout and equals the surroundings.
2. Mechanical equilibrium: Mechanical properties (pressure) are uniform; no part does work on another.
3. Chemical equilibrium: Chemical composition is uniform with no net chemical reaction.

Thermodynamic Processes

When a thermodynamic system changes from one state to another, the operation is called a Process.

Process Type Constant Condition Mathematical Condition Example / Key Feature
Isothermal Temperature (T) dT = 0 Carried out in a thermostat (constant-temperature bath).
Adiabatic Heat exchange (q) dq = 0 Insulated container (thermos), high vacuum, polished surfaces.
Isobaric Pressure (P) dP = 0 Heating/vaporization of water at atmospheric pressure.
Isochoric Volume (V) dV = 0 Heating a substance inside a rigid, non-expanding chamber.
Cyclic Initial & Final States match dE = 0, dH = 0 System undergoes multiple steps and returns to its exact initial state.

Reversible vs. Irreversible Processes

  • Reversible Process: Occurs infinitesimally slowly through an infinite series of equilibrium steps (e.g., decreasing external pressure on a frictionless piston by dP). At any instant, an infinitesimal change reverses its direction.
  • Irreversible Process: Proceeds from initial to final state in a single rapid step (e.g., sudden drop in external pressure) and cannot be reversed along the same path. The system is in equilibrium only at the initial and final states.
Reversible Process Irreversible Process
1. Occurs in infinite infinitesimally small steps; takes infinite time. 1. Occurs rapidly in finite time.
2. Ideal/imaginary (assumes frictionless, weightless piston). 2. Real and can actually be performed in practice.
3. System stays in equilibrium at all intermediate stages. 3. System is in equilibrium only at the initial and final stages.
4. All changes are completely reversed if direction is reversed. 4. Changes do not return to initial state by themselves.
5. Proceeds extremely slowly. 5. Proceeds at a measurable speed.
6. Work done is greater (maximum) than irreversible process. 6. Work done is smaller than corresponding reversible process.

Nature of Heat, Work & Pressure–Volume Work

Units & Sign Conventions

Units of Work & Heat
  • Work: Force × distance. CGS unit = erg; SI unit = joule (J).
  • 1 J = 107 ergs  |  1 kJ = 1000 J
  • Heat: Traditional unit = calorie (cal) (heat to raise 1 g water by 1 °C near 15 °C); SI unit = joule (J).
  • 1 cal = 4.184 J  |  1 J = 0.2390 cal
Sign Conventions (Book Summary, p. 260)
  • Heat absorbed by system: +q
  • Heat given off by system: −q
  • Work done on system (compression): +w
  • Work done by system (expansion): −w

Pressure–Volume (PV) Work at Constant External Pressure

Consider a gas expanding in a cylinder with a frictionless piston of area A against a constant external pressure Pext. Always use Pext (not internal gas pressure) to calculate PV work.

Formula: Constant-Pressure PV Work
w = −F × l = −(Pext × A) × l = −Pext ΔV = −Pext (V2 − V1)
For expansion (V2 > V1), w is negative (work done by system). For compression (V2 < V1), w is positive (work done on system).
Solved Problem 1: PV Work at Constant External Pressure

Problem: Calculate the PV work done when a gas expands from 0.001 m³ to 0.002 m³ against a constant external pressure of 1013250.00 Pa. Express in joules and calories.

Solution:
w = −Pext(V2 − V1) = −1013250 × (0.002 − 0.001) = −1013.25 J
In calories: −1013.25 / 4.184 = −242.17 cal.

Isothermal Reversible vs. Irreversible Expansion of an Ideal Gas

In an isothermal reversible expansion, Pext is reduced by infinitesimal amounts dP so at every step Pext ≈ Pgas = nRT/V:

Formulas: Isothermal Expansion Work (Ideal Gas)
Reversible:   wrev = −∫V1V2 P dV = −nRT lnV2V1 = −2.303 nRT logV2V1 = −2.303 nRT logP1P2
Irreversible (sudden drop to P2):   wirr = −P2(V2 − V1)
Note on textbook sign usage: In Solved Problems 2, 3, 14, and 17, the textbook omits the leading minus sign when asking for "work done by the gas" (reporting magnitude as positive). For compression, the sign reverses.
Figure: Work is a path function. Between the same states A and B, shaded area (a) Wrev is strictly greater than (b) Wirr.
Why Reversible Work is Always Maximum (Proof) Expansion work depends on Pext—larger Pext means more work done by the gas. However, Pext cannot exceed Pgas, or compression would occur instead. Thus, the largest possible value of Pext for expansion is Pext = Pgas, which defines a reversible expansion. Therefore, |wrev| = wmax. Also, because wrev ≠ wirr between identical states, mechanical work is a path function, not a state function.
Solved Problems 2, 3, 14 & 17: Isothermal Reversible Expansion Calculations

Solved Problem 2: 1 mol ideal gas at 298 K expands reversibly from 0.01 m³ to 0.02 m³.
|w| = 2.303 × 1 × 8.314 × 298 × log(0.02/0.01) = 5705.85 × 0.3010 = 1717.46 J (410.48 cal).

Solved Problem 3: 1 mol ideal gas expands reversibly & isothermally from 5 atm (506625 Pa) to 1 atm (101325 Pa) at 298 K.
|w| = 2.303 × 1 × 8.314 × 298 × log(5/1) = 5705.85 × 0.6990 = 3988.2 J.

Solved Problem 14: 0.044 kg Neon (at. mass = 20 ⇒ n = 44/20 = 2.2 mol) expands reversibly at 300 K from 1013250 Pa to 202650 Pa (R = 8.3736).
|w| = 2.303 × 2.2 × 8.3736 × 300 × log(5) = 8896.28 J.

Solved Problem 17: 2 mol N2 expands isothermally & reversibly from 0.01 m³ to 0.02 m³ at 298 K.
|w| = 2.303 × 2 × 8.314 × 298 × log(2) = 11411.69 × 0.3010 = 3434.92 J.

Internal Energy & First Law of Thermodynamics

Internal Energy (E or U) The total of all possible kinds of energy within a system (translational, rotational, vibrational kinetic energy of molecules, plus kinetic and potential energy of nuclei and electrons).
  • It is a state function and an extensive property.
  • Absolute value cannot be measured; only change ΔE = Ef − Ein is measured.
  • SI Unit: joule (J); non-SI: calorie (1 cal = 4.184 J).

First Law of Thermodynamics

An application of the Law of Conservation of Energy:

  • Total energy of an isolated system remains constant, though it may change from one form to another.
  • Equivalent statements: (1) Whenever energy of one type disappears, an equivalent amount of another type is produced. (2) Total energy of system + surroundings is conserved. (3) It is impossible to construct a perpetual motion machine that produces work without spending energy.
Mathematical Statement of the First Law
ΔE = q − w   or   ΔE = q − PΔV
Important Sign Note: In this equation (used in the textbook's First Law section and Solved Problems 5, 6, and MCQ 47), w represents work done by the system (w = PΔV > 0 for expansion). If using the strict IUPAC convention where expansion work itself carries a negative sign (wIUPAC = −PΔV), the law is written ΔE = q + wIUPAC. Both give identical physical values for ΔE.

Four Special Cases of the First Law (ΔE = q − w)

Case 1: Cyclic Isothermal Ideal Gas ΔE = 0 ⇒ q = w
All heat absorbed is converted into work done.
Case 2: Isochoric Process (ΔV = 0) w = 0 ⇒ ΔE = qv
Heat absorbed at constant volume equals increase in internal energy.
Case 3: Adiabatic Process (q = 0) ΔE = −w
Work done by the system decreases its internal energy (or work done on the system increases internal energy).
Case 4: Isobaric Process (P = const) ΔE = q − PΔV
Heat transferred changes both internal energy and performs PV work.
Solved Problems 4, 5, 6 & 7: First Law Calculations

Solved Problem 4: 2 mol H2 at 303975 Pa expands isothermally & reversibly at 323 K to 101325 Pa. Find ΔE, q, w.
Isothermal ideal gas ⇒ ΔE = 0. Thus q = w.
w = −2 × 8.314 × 323 × 2.303 × log(303975 / 101325) = −5903.387 J; book writes q = −5903.387 J (note: physically, 5903.39 J of heat is absorbed by the expanding gas so q = +5903.39 J and work done by gas is 5903.39 J).

Solved Problem 5: 0.001 kg water at 373 K boils to steam (Vsteam = 0.001671 m³, Vwater ≈ 10−6 m³) at 101325 Pa. Latent heat = 2260980 J/kg. Find ΔE.
q = 0.001 × 2260980 = 2260.98 J.
w = PΔV = 101325 × (0.001671 − 0.000001) ≈ 169.21 J.
ΔE = q − w = 2260.98 − 169.21 = 2091.77 J.

Solved Problem 6: Gas expands against 101325 Pa from 0.005 m³ to 0.01 m³, absorbing 400 J heat. Find ΔE.
w = 101325 × (0.01 − 0.005) = 506.625 J.
ΔE = q − w = 400 − 506.625 = −106.625 J.

Solved Problem 7: 0.01 kg H2 (5 mol) expands isothermally & reversibly from 2026500 Pa to 101325 Pa at 273 K.
wmax = 2.303 × 5 × 8.314 × 273 × log(20) = 34.00 kJ; Ideal gas isothermal ⇒ ΔE = 0, q = 34.00 kJ.

Enthalpy of a System (H) & Relation Between ΔH and ΔE

Definition: Enthalpy (H) The total heat content of a system at constant pressure, equivalent to its internal energy plus PV energy. Since E, P, and V are state functions, Enthalpy (H) is also a state function (and an extensive property).
Key Enthalpy Equations
H = E + PV
At constant pressure:   ΔH = ΔE + PΔV = qp
For gaseous reactions:   ΔH = ΔE + Δn RT   (where Δn = nproducts(g) − nreactants(g))

Units & Sign Conventions of ΔH

  • Endothermic process (H2 > H1): ΔH is +ve (heat absorbed).
  • Exothermic process (H1 > H2): ΔH is −ve (heat evolved), e.g., H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l) + 286.042 kJ (ΔH = −286.042 kJ).
  • Units: kcal or kJ (SI unit).
  • When does ΔH = ΔE? When Δn = 0 (e.g., H2 + I2 ⇌ 2HI), or for reactions involving only solids/liquids (ΔV ≈ 0).

Derivation of ΔH = ΔE + Δn RT

Volumes of solids/liquids are negligible compared to gases. If a reaction starts with n1 moles of gas and ends with n2 moles of gas at constant P and T: PV1 = n1RT and PV2 = n2RT. Thus PΔV = (n2 − n1)RT = Δn RT. Substituting into ΔH = ΔE + PΔV gives ΔH = ΔE + Δn RT.

Solved Problems 8 & 13: Calculating ΔH and ΔE

Solved Problem 8: For H2F2(g) → H2(g) + F2(g), ΔE = −59.4526 kJ/mol at 298 K. Find ΔH.
Δn = 2 − 1 = 1.
Δn RT = 1 × 8.314 × 298 = 2477.572 J/mol = 2.4776 kJ/mol.
ΔH = −59.4526 + 2.4776 = −56.975 kJ/mol. (Note: The textbook omitted converting J to kJ and printed −2418.12 kJ/mol; always match units!)

Solved Problem 13: At 298 K, combustion of 1 mol liquid benzene: C6H6(l) + 7½ O2(g) → 6 CO2(g) + 3 H2O(l), ΔH = −3269807 J. Find ΔE.
Δn = 6 − 7.5 = −1.5.
ΔE = ΔH − Δn RT = −3269807 − (−1.5)(8.314 × 298) = −3269807 + 3716.36 = −3266.09 kJ.

Molar Heat Capacities (Cp and Cv)

Definitions
  • Heat Capacity (c): Heat absorbed by unit mass to raise its temperature by 1 K (or 1 °C): c = q / [m(T2 − T1)].
  • Molar Heat Capacity (C): Amount of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 mole of a substance by 1 K: C = dq / dT.
  • Units: J K−1 mol−1 (SI) or cal K−1 mol−1.
  • Important: Like heat, heat capacity is NOT a state function; the path (constant V or constant P) must be specified.
Molar Heat Capacities & Thermodynamic Relations
At Constant Volume (dV = 0):   Cv = ∂E∂TV   (rate of change of E with T)
At Constant Pressure (dP = 0):   Cp = ∂H∂TP   (rate of change of H with T)
Mayer's Relation (1 mol ideal gas):   Cp − Cv = R  (8.314 J K−1 mol−1 = 1.987 cal K−1 mol−1)
For n moles of ideal gas:   ΔE = n Cv (T2 − T1)   |   ΔH = n Cp (T2 − T1)
Why is Cₚ always greater than Cᵥ? + Derivation of Cₚ − Cᵥ = R

Physical Reason: At constant volume, all absorbed heat raises the internal energy (temperature) because no expansion work is done. At constant pressure, part of the absorbed heat increases internal energy and the rest is spent doing PV expansion work. Hence, more heat is needed per degree rise at constant pressure: Cp > Cv.

Derivation: For 1 mole of an ideal gas, H = E + PV = E + RT.
Differentiating with respect to T:
dH/dT = dE/dT + R ⇒ Cp = Cv + R ⇒ Cp − Cv = R.

Solved Problems 9/16, 10, 11 & 15: ΔE, ΔH, and Heat Calculations

Solved Problem 16 (and 9): Heating 0.064 kg O2 (n = 64/32 = 2 mol) from 273 K to 373 K (Cv = 20.93, Cp = 29.308 J mol−1 K−1):
ΔE = 2 × 20.93 × (373 − 273) = 4186 J
ΔH = 2 × 29.308 × (373 − 273) = 5861.6 J. (Note: SP 9 in the book mistakenly used n = 4 instead of 2.)

Solved Problem 10: Heat to raise 0.2135 kg water (n = 213.5/18 = 11.86 mol) from 298 K to 373 K (Cp = 75.36 J mol−1 K−1):
q = 11.86 × 75.36 × (373 − 298) ≈ 67,038 J (67.04 kJ).

Solved Problem 11: 3 mol ideal gas (Cv = 20.93, R = 8.314) heated from 273 K to 323 K.
ΔE = 3 × 20.93 × 50 = 3139.5 J; Cp = 20.93 + 8.314 = 29.244 ⇒ ΔH = 3 × 29.244 × 50 = 4386.6 J.

Solved Problem 15: 1 mol Argon heated from 313 K to 373 K (Cv = 12.56, Cp = 20.93):
(i) Constant V: ΔE = 1 × 12.56 × 60 = 753.6 J; (ii) Constant P: ΔH = 1 × 20.93 × 60 = 1255.8 J.

Joule–Thomson Effect (Joule–Kelvin Effect)

Definition The phenomenon of producing a lowering of temperature when a gas expands adiabatically from a region of high pressure through a porous plug into a region of low pressure.
Figure: Joule–Thomson Experiment. Since q = 0 and w = P₂V₂ − P₁V₁, enthalpy stays constant (ΔH = 0).

Proof that Joule–Thomson Expansion is Isenthalpic (ΔH = 0)

Work done on gas at piston A = P1V1; work done by gas at piston B = P2V2. Net work done by gas: w = P2V2 − P1V1. Since the tube is insulated, q = 0.
By First Law: ΔE = E2 − E1 = −w = P1V1 − P2V2 ⇒ E2 + P2V2 = E1 + P1V1 ⇒ H2 = H1 (ΔH = 0).

Joule–Thomson Coefficient (μ) & Inversion Temperature
μ = ∂T∂PH   (temperature change per atm drop in pressure at constant H)
  • μ > 0: Gas cools on expansion (most gases at room temperature; used for liquefaction).
  • μ < 0: Gas warms on expansion (e.g., H2 and He at room temperature).
  • μ = 0: For an ideal gas (since PV is constant at constant T and molecules have no intermolecular forces).
  • Inversion Temperature: Temperature at which μ changes sign. Gases must be cooled below their inversion temperature before they cool on Joule–Thomson expansion (text notes H2 inversion point as −193.15 K, physically ~193–202 K / −80 °C).

Molecular Explanation: Since H = E + PV is constant, any increase in PV during expansion (as in ordinary gases) must reduce internal energy E (overcoming intermolecular attractions), causing cooling (T2 < T1). For H2 and He at room temperature, PV decreases as pressure drops, so E increases and T2 > T1.

Adiabatic Expansion of an Ideal Gas & Work Done

In an adiabatic process (q = 0), expansion work is done strictly at the expense of internal energy (dE = −P dV), so temperature must fall during expansion.

Adiabatic Relations (Ideal Gas, γ = Cp/Cv)
T1V1γ−1 = T2V2γ−1   (TVγ−1 = constant)
P1V1γ = P2V2γ   (PVγ = constant)   |   T2T1γγ−1 = P2P1
Adiabatic Reversible Work (n moles):   wmax = nR(T2 − T1)1 − γ = nR(T1 − T2)γ − 1 = −n Cv(T2 − T1)
Sign interpretation in textbook: When T2 > T1 (compression), wmax is negative (work done on gas); when T2 < T1 (expansion), wmax is positive (work done by gas).
Step-by-Step Derivations: TV^(γ−1) = const, PV^γ = const, and Adiabatic Work

1. Deriving TVγ−1 = constant:
For 1 mol ideal gas, dE = Cv dT = −P dV = −(RT/V)dV.
Rearranging: Cv(dT/T) = −R(dV/V).
Integrating between (T1, V1) and (T2, V2): Cv ln(T2/T1) = R ln(V1/V2).
Since R/Cv = (Cp − Cv)/Cv = γ − 1, we get ln(T2/T1) = (γ − 1) ln(V1/V2) ⇒ T1V1γ−1 = T2V2γ−1.

2. Deriving PVγ = constant:
Using T2/T1 = (P2V2)/(P1V1) = (V1/V2)γ−1 gives P1V1γ = P2V2γ.

3. Deriving Adiabatic Work:
Differentiating PVγ = const gives VγdP + γPVγ−1dV = 0 ⇒ VdP = −γPdV.
Differentiating PV = RT gives PdV + VdP = RdT ⇒ VdP = RdT − PdV.
Equating both: −γPdV = RdT − PdV ⇒ (1 − γ)PdV = RdT ⇒ dw = PdV = RdT/(1 − γ).
Integrating for 1 mole from T1 to T2: wmax = R(T2 − T1)/(1 − γ).

Isothermal vs. Adiabatic Expansion Curves

Figure: For a monatomic ideal gas (γ = 1.67), the adiabatic curve is steeper because both V increases and T falls, causing P to drop faster.
Solved Problem 12: Adiabatic Reversible Expansion of 2 Moles of Ideal Gas

Problem: 2 moles of monatomic ideal gas (γ = 5/3 = 1.67) at 273.2 K and 2026500 Pa expand adiabatically & reversibly to 202650 Pa. Calculate w.

Correct Thermodynamic Solution:
Step 1: (T2 / T1)γ/(γ−1) = P2 / P1 ⇒ (T2 / 273.2)5/2 = 202650 / 2026500 = 0.1
T2 = 273.2 × (0.1)0.4 = 273.2 × 0.3981 = 108.76 K.
Step 2 (Work formula or −ΔE): w = 2 × 8.314 × (108.76 − 273.2) / (1 − 5/3) = +4101.6 J.
(Note: The textbook printed an exponent of 5/3 instead of 5/2 in Step 1; using the exact 5/2 exponent ensures full exam accuracy.)
