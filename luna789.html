Start here: one consistent sign convention

Use Q for heat transferred to the system and Wby for work done by the system. This makes expansion work positive as an output.

ΔE = Q − Wby   =   Q + won, where won = −Wby
  • Heat: Q > 0 enters the system; Q < 0 leaves it.
  • Work: Wby > 0 when the system does work; won > 0 when surroundings do work on it.
  • Thus, expansion has Wby > 0 and won < 0; compression has the opposite signs.
Notation alert. The supplied textbook extraction switches between “work by the system” and “work on the system” in some equations and contains a few OCR/arithmetic errors. This guide states both conventions explicitly and uses the consistent equation ΔE = Q − Wby. If your teacher uses the chemistry convention w = work on the system, use ΔE = Q + w.

First Law of Thermodynamics — Basic Concepts

Energy accounting, systems, processes, work, internal energy, enthalpy, and heat capacity

1. What thermodynamics studies

Thermodynamics studies heat and other forms of energy entering or leaving a system as it undergoes a physical or chemical change. Changes in measurable properties—temperature, pressure, volume, and composition—help us determine energy changes, heat, and work.

The subject is based on broad empirical generalizations called the first, second, and third laws. They are supported by experiment and do not depend on a particular theory of atomic or molecular structure.

2. Scope and limitations

Scope

  1. Many physical-chemistry laws (including the van ’t Hoff law of lowering of vapour pressure, the Phase Rule, and the Distribution Law) can be derived from thermodynamics.
  2. It predicts whether a physical or chemical change is possible under stated temperature, pressure, and concentration conditions.
  3. It predicts how far a change proceeds before equilibrium is reached.

Limitations

  1. It treats macroscopic bulk systems; it does not describe individual atoms or molecules or their internal structure.
  2. It does not give the rate of a physical change or reaction. It mainly compares initial and final equilibrium states.

3. System, boundary, surroundings, and phases

System: the part of the universe selected for study. Surroundings: everything outside it. Boundary: the real or imaginary surface separating the two.

Examples: water in a beaker is the system; the beaker and nearby air are surroundings. Oxygen in a piston-cylinder is the system; the cylinder, piston, and outside objects are surroundings.

Three thermodynamic system types

Adiabatic system/process: no thermal energy (heat) passes into or out of the system: Q = 0. An adiabatic system need not be isolated: it may still do work, and an open adiabatic system may exchange matter.

Homogeneous and heterogeneous systems

  • Homogeneous: uniform throughout and consists of one phase. Examples: a pure solid, liquid, or gas; a gas mixture; a true solution of a solid in a liquid.
  • Phase: a homogeneous, physically distinct, mechanically separable part of a system.
  • Heterogeneous: nonuniform and contains two or more phases. Ice–water has two phases; ice–water–vapour has three.

4. Properties and state of a system

Intensive versus extensive properties
Intensive — independent of amountExtensive — depends on amount and is additive
Temperature, pressure, density, concentrationMass, volume, number of moles
Boiling point, freezing point, surface tensionInternal energy, enthalpy, entropy
Refractive index, viscosityGibbs free energy

Doubling the amount of a system doubles an extensive property such as volume or internal energy; it does not double an intensive property such as temperature or density.

State: the condition of a system when its properties are fixed. Pressure (P), temperature (T), volume (V), mass, and composition are state variables (also called state functions or thermodynamic parameters).

For one mole of a pure ideal gas, the equation of state is PV = RT; for n moles, PV = nRT. If composition is fixed and two independent variables are specified, the equation of state fixes the third. For example, specifying P and T fixes V.

  • Independent state variables: the variables chosen to define the state.
  • Dependent state variable: a variable determined from the independent ones and the equation of state.
  • A state function’s change depends only on the initial and final states, not on the route. For example, heating water from 273.15 K to 298.15 K gives ΔT = 25.00 K by any route.
State versus path: internal energy, enthalpy, and entropy are state functions. Heat and work depend on the process and are path functions.

5. Equilibrium and non-equilibrium

  • Thermodynamic equilibrium: state variables have uniform, constant values throughout each phase.
  • Non-equilibrium: state variables differ from one part of the system to another. Rapidly compressing a gas can heat the region near the piston while the far end remains cooler.

Thermodynamics describes equilibrium states; a rapid process may pass through non-equilibrium states that cannot be assigned one uniform P and T.

Equilibrium criteria: (1) uniform system temperature, equal to the surroundings at thermal equilibrium; (2) uniform mechanical properties, with no internal region doing net work on another; (3) uniform chemical composition and no net chemical change. In a heterogeneous system, each phase has constant state variables.

7. Reversible and irreversible processes

Reversible process: an ideal limiting process carried out through infinitely many infinitesimal steps, with the system in equilibrium at every stage. An infinitesimal change in conditions can reverse its direction.

For gas expansion, the external and internal pressures are almost equal at every step. Lowering the external pressure by an infinitesimal dP allows an infinitesimal expansion; raising it by dP reverses the process. The ideal model assumes a weightless, frictionless piston.

Irreversible process: a real process that proceeds through finite differences (often in one rapid step). The system is generally in equilibrium only before and after the change, not at intermediate points.

8. Heat, work, units, and signs

When a system changes state, energy may cross the boundary as heat or as mechanical work. Mechanical work is force × distance. In elementary thermodynamics, the most common work is pressure–volume (PV) expansion or compression work.

9. Pressure–volume work

Work is evaluated using the external pressure, Pext, against which the system moves. For a piston of area A moving a distance l, ΔV = A·l.

Wby = ∫V₁V₂ Pext dV   ;   won = −∫V₁V₂ Pext dV
Constant external pressure:   Wby = Pext(V₂ − V₁),   won = −PextΔV

Expansion gives positive work by the gas, and compression gives negative work by the gas. The work is path-dependent, not a state function.

Isothermal reversible expansion of an ideal gas

At each infinitesimal reversible step, Pext ≈ Pgas = P. For n moles at constant T, P = nRT/V.

Wby,rev = ∫P dV = nRT ln(V₂/V₁) = nRT ln(P₁/P₂)
won,rev = −nRT ln(V₂/V₁) = −nRT ln(P₁/P₂)
log10 form: ln x = 2.303 log10x

Use absolute temperature in kelvin. The units of R determine the work units. For an ideal gas, an isothermal change has ΔE = 0, so Q = Wby.

Isothermal irreversible expansion

If the external pressure is dropped suddenly and remains constant at Pext during expansion:

Wby,irr = Pext(V₂ − V₁)
If Pext = P₂, then Wby,irr = P₂(V₂ − V₁)

Why reversible expansion gives maximum work

  1. Work depends on the external pressure at each volume change.
  2. The greatest opposing external pressure that still permits expansion is infinitesimally below the gas pressure.
  3. A reversible expansion keeps those pressures almost equal at each step, so the gas pushes against the largest possible pressure throughout.
Result: for identical initial and final states, Wby,rev > Wby,irr. With the “work on the system” convention, won,rev is more negative.

10. Internal energy and the first law

Internal energy, E (also written U): the total microscopic energy of the system, including molecular translational, rotational, and vibrational energies and the kinetic and potential energies of nuclei and electrons.

Internal energy is a state function and an extensive property. Its absolute value is not normally needed; changes are used:

ΔE = Efinal − Einitial

ΔE is positive if the final energy is higher and negative if it is lower. Its SI unit is the joule; 1 cal = 4.184 J.

First law: energy is conserved. The total energy of an isolated system remains constant, although energy can change form. Equivalently, energy cannot be created or destroyed.
ΔE = Q − Wby   (or ΔE = Q + won)

The internal-energy change of a closed system equals heat supplied to it minus work it does. The surroundings and system together conserve energy.

  • Whenever one form of energy disappears, an equivalent amount of another form is produced.
  • The energy of system plus surroundings is conserved.
  • A perpetual-motion machine that produces work without an energy input is impossible.
Useful first-law special cases
ConditionResultMeaning
CyclicΔE = 0; Qnet = Wby,netEnergy returns to its initial value.

AdiabaticQ = 0; ΔE = −WbyWork comes at the expense of internal energy during expansion.

For an ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature. An isothermal ideal-gas process therefore has ΔE = 0. In free expansion into a vacuum, Q = 0 and W = 0; hence ΔE = 0 and the ideal gas temperature does not change.

11. Enthalpy and the relation between ΔH and ΔE

Enthalpy: a state function defined by H = E + PV. At constant pressure it is convenient for describing heat changes because it includes the internal energy and the PV term.
ΔH = H₂ − H₁ = ΔE + Δ(PV)
At constant pressure:   ΔH = ΔE + PΔV

For a process at constant pressure involving only PV work, Wby = PΔV. From the first law, ΔE = Qp − PΔV; substituting into ΔH gives:

ΔH = Qp

Thus constant-pressure heat equals enthalpy change when only PV work is involved. At constant volume, Qv = ΔE. Positive ΔH means heat absorbed (endothermic); negative ΔH means heat released (exothermic). For reactions of only liquids and solids, ΔV is usually tiny, so ΔH ≈ ΔE.

Gas-reaction relation: ΔH = ΔE + ΔngRT

For ideal-gas reactants and products, the gas volume change at constant T and P is:

PΔV = (nproducts,g − nreactants,g)RT = ΔngRT
ΔH = ΔE + ΔngRT

Count only gaseous species when finding Δng; use stoichiometric coefficients in the balanced equation. R and energy units must match.

12. Molar heat capacity; CV, CP, and Mayer’s relation

Heat capacity: heat required to raise a system’s temperature by one degree under a specified process. Molar heat capacity: heat needed to raise one mole by 1 K.
For a finite temperature interval: C = Q/(T₂ − T₁); for a small change: C = δQ/dT
For n moles with nearly constant C: Q = nCΔT

Because heat is path-dependent, the heating condition must be specified. Units for molar heat capacity include J mol−1 K−1 and cal mol−1 K−1.

Derivation at constant volume

For reversible PV work, δQ = dE + P dV. At constant volume dV = 0, so δQv = dE:

CV = (∂E/∂T)V   per mole
For n moles:   CV,total = (∂E/∂T)V, with dE = nCVdT

Thus constant-volume heat raises internal energy; no PV expansion work is done.

Derivation at constant pressure

Since H = E + PV, differentiating at constant pressure gives dH/dT = dE/dT + P(dV/dT). The first law shows that this is the heat capacity at constant pressure:

CP = (∂H/∂T)P   per mole

At constant pressure some supplied heat raises internal energy and some supplies expansion work. For the same temperature increase, generally CP > CV.

Derive CP − CV = R for one mole of an ideal gas

  • H = E + PV, and for one mole PV = RT; therefore H = E + RT.
  • Differentiate with respect to T: dH/dT = dE/dT + R.
  • Use CP = dH/dT and CV = dE/dT.
    • CP − CV = R   (molar ideal-gas heat capacities)

    Energy and enthalpy changes when heat capacities are constant

    For n moles of an ideal gas:
    ΔE = nCV(T₂ − T₁)
    ΔH = nCP(T₂ − T₁) = ΔE + nR(T₂ − T₁)

    13. Joule–Thomson (Joule–Kelvin) effect

    When a compressed real gas is forced from high pressure through a porous plug into a lower-pressure region in an insulated apparatus, its temperature often changes. This is the Joule–Thomson effect.

    Experiment and result

    An insulated tube has a porous plug in the middle, pistons on each side, and thermometers. Gas at P₁ is pushed through the plug and emerges at P₂. Most gases cool; hydrogen and helium can warm at ordinary room temperature.

    The throttling process is adiabatic. The pushing work at the high-pressure side and expansion work at the low-pressure side give the energy balance:

    E₁ + P₁V₁ = E₂ + P₂V₂
    H₁ = H₂;   ΔH = 0
    Joule–Thomson coefficient: μJT = (∂T/∂P)H, the temperature change per pressure change in an isenthalpic throttling process.
    • μJT > 0: reducing pressure (dP < 0) cools the gas.
    • μJT < 0: reducing pressure warms the gas.
    • The inversion temperature is where the coefficient changes sign. Most gases cool at room temperature; hydrogen and helium must first be cooled below their inversion region to be liquefied by further throttling.

    At constant H, a rise in PV must be balanced by a fall in E, often causing cooling. The effect is a real-gas effect; an ideal gas has enthalpy dependent only on T and has no Joule–Thomson temperature change in an ideal throttle.

    14. Reversible adiabatic expansion of an ideal gas

    In an adiabatic process Q = 0. From the first law, ΔE = −Wby. During expansion, the gas uses internal energy to do work, so its temperature falls.

    Derive TVγ−1 = constant

  • For one mole during a small reversible expansion, dE = CVdT and dE = −P dV.
  • Use P = RT/V: CVdT = −RT(dV/V).
  • Divide by T and integrate from state 1 to state 2: CV ln(T₂/T₁) = −R ln(V₂/V₁).
  • For an ideal gas, CP − CV = R; define γ = CP/CV, so R/CV = γ − 1.
    • ln(T₂/T₁) = −(γ − 1)ln(V₂/V₁)
    TVγ−1 = constant
    PVγ = constant
    T₂/T₁ = (V₁/V₂)γ−1 = (P₂/P₁)(γ−1)/γ
    P₂/P₁ = (V₁/V₂)γ

    γ = CP/CV; for a monatomic ideal gas γ ≈ 1.67. An isothermal ideal gas follows PV = constant, while an adiabatic ideal gas follows PVγ = constant. The adiabatic P–V curve is steeper: in an isothermal expansion heat enters to replace work; in an adiabatic expansion the gas cools.

    Work in a reversible adiabatic change

    Wby = nCV(T₁ − T₂) = nR(T₁ − T₂)/(γ − 1)
    won = nCV(T₂ − T₁)

    For expansion T₂ < T₁, so Wby is positive. For compression T₂ > T₁, work is done on the gas.

    Thermochemistry

    Heat changes in reactions, phase changes, Hess’s law, and calorimetry

    1. Meaning, energy changes, and history

    Thermochemistry is the branch of physical chemistry concerned with heat changes caused by chemical reactions and physical transformations.

    Internal energy cannot usually be measured absolutely, but its change can be measured. For a reaction, ΔE = Eproducts − Ereactants. Energy changes often reflect old bonds breaking and new bonds forming; thermochemistry therefore helps estimate bond energies.

    • 1782–1783: Antoine Lavoisier and Pierre-Simon Laplace used an ice calorimeter and showed that heat evolved in a reaction is equal in magnitude to heat absorbed by its reverse reaction. They also studied specific and latent heats and combustion.
    • 1840: Germain Hess formulated the law that the total heat change is the same whether a change happens in one step or several. Hess’s law follows from conservation of energy.

    Common units are cal, kcal, J, and kJ; 1 kcal = 1000 cal, 1 cal ≈ 4.184 J, and 1 kcal ≈ 4.184 kJ.

    2. Exothermic and endothermic changes

    Exothermic

    Heat leaves the system; surroundings warm. Products have lower enthalpy than reactants.

    ΔH < 0

    Endothermic

    Heat enters the system; surroundings cool. Products have higher enthalpy than reactants.

    ΔH > 0
    ΔH = Hproducts − Hreactants
    Released heat: exothermic, negative ΔH (or ΔE at constant volume).
    Absorbed heat: endothermic, positive ΔH (or ΔE at constant volume).

    3. Thermochemical equations and heat of reaction

    A thermochemical equation gives the balanced chemical equation and its heat change. The heat amount applies to the quantities written in the equation. If the equation is multiplied by a factor, ΔH is multiplied by the same factor.

    • Balance the equation.
    • Give ΔH or ΔE for the quantities shown.
    • State physical phases: (s), (l), (g), and (aq).
    • Temperature and pressure matter; state them when relevant.

    For example, formation of liquid water and water vapour have different enthalpy changes:

    H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l)   ΔH ≈ −286.0 kJ mol⁻¹
    H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(g)   ΔH ≈ −242.0 kJ mol⁻¹

    Heat of reaction is the heat absorbed or evolved when the reactants in the balanced equation react as written. At constant pressure it is ΔH; at constant volume it is ΔE. A standard reaction enthalpy, ΔH°, in this chapter is referenced to 298 K and 1 atm (101,325 Pa).

    4. Relation between heat at constant pressure and volume

    At constant volume no PV expansion work is done, so Qv = ΔE. At constant pressure the system may expand or contract, so the heat is ΔH:

    ΔH = ΔE + PΔV
    For ideal gases in a reaction: ΔH = ΔE + ΔngRT

    The gas-mole correction is usually important in reactions involving gases; liquid and solid volume changes are often negligible.

    5. How reaction heat changes with temperature: Kirchhoff equations

    Reaction heat varies with temperature because reactants and products have different heat capacities. Let ΔCV = ΣCV,products − ΣCV,reactants, with stoichiometric coefficients included. Similarly define ΔCP.

    (∂ΔE/∂T)V = ΔCV
    ΔE₂ − ΔE₁ = ∫T₁T₂ ΔCVdT
    If ΔCV is constant:   ΔE₂ = ΔE₁ + ΔCV(T₂ − T₁)
    (∂ΔH/∂T)P = ΔCP
    ΔH₂ − ΔH₁ = ∫T₁T₂ ΔCPdT
    If ΔCP is constant:   ΔH₂ = ΔH₁ + ΔCP(T₂ − T₁)

    These are Kirchhoff’s equations; they transfer known reaction heats to another temperature when the heat capacities are known.

    Heat of formation and standard heat of formation

    Standard heat of formation, ΔH°f: formation of one mole of compound from elements, all in their standard states (here, 298 K and 1 atm). By convention ΔH°f of an element in its standard state is zero.

    Heat of combustion and its uses

    Heat of combustion, ΔHc: enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burnt in excess oxygen or air. Combustion is exothermic, so ΔHc is negative.

    Example: CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l), ΔHc ≈ −890.3 kJ mol⁻¹. Carbon’s complete combustion is C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), about −394 kJ mol⁻¹; forming CO instead is incomplete oxidation, not the full heat of combustion.

    1. Find heats of formation: combustion data can be measured readily and combined with Hess’s law.
    2. Compare fuels and foods: calorific value is heat released when 1 g is completely burnt; units include cal g⁻¹, kcal g⁻¹, or kJ kg⁻¹. Methane gives about 890.3/16 = 55.6 kJ g⁻¹; ethane gives about 1559.7/30 = 52.0 kJ g⁻¹.
    3. Help determine constitution: approximate additivity of combustion heats and atom/linkage contributions can test likely structures. The chapter cites support for Kekulé’s alternating single/double-bond formula for benzene.

    In a homologous series, successive combustion heats differ by nearly a constant amount; the chapter gives about 661 J for the relevant series discussed.

    Heat of solution

    Heat of solution: enthalpy change when one mole of a substance dissolves in a specified amount of solvent at a stated temperature. For a value independent of solvent amount, define it at infinite dilution—further dilution causes no additional heat effect.

    Dissolving an ionic solid involves separating ions (often absorbs heat) and hydrating them (often releases heat). The balance determines the net heat. The excerpt gives CuSO₄ dissolving to a 1 M solution as absorbing about 78.5 kJ, and lists KCl(aq) and MgSO₄(aq) examples with reported changes of −18.42 and −84.91 kJ; concentration and sign convention must be stated when using such values.

    Heat of neutralization and dissociation

    Heat of neutralization: enthalpy change when one gram-equivalent of acid reacts with one gram-equivalent of base in dilute solution.

    For a strong acid and strong base, the net ionic reaction is H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l), with a typical heat near −57.3 kJ per mole of water formed. The values for different strong acid/strong base pairs in the chapter cluster around −57.3 to −58.1 kJ.

    Weak acids or bases need energy to ionize, so their measured neutralization heat is usually less exothermic. The chapter lists examples near −51.50 kJ and −49.82 kJ. For NH₄OH neutralized by a strong acid, the difference from −57.36 kJ gives a dissociation heat of about +5.86 kJ mol⁻¹.

    Heat of dissociation: enthalpy change when one mole of a weak acid or base dissociates into ions.

    7. Hess’s law and Lavoisier–Laplace law

    Hess’s law of constant heat summation: if a chemical change can occur by different routes—one step or several steps—the total heat or enthalpy change is the same, provided the initial and final states are the same.

    Because ΔE and ΔH are state functions, the route does not matter. If direct conversion A→Z releases Q₁ and a staged route A→B→C→Z releases q₁+q₂+q₃, then Q₁ = q₁+q₂+q₃. If not, repeating a cycle could produce energy indefinitely, violating the first law.

    Lavoisier–Laplace law: the heat evolved in a chemical reaction equals the heat absorbed in the reverse reaction in magnitude; the signs are opposite. If A→B has ΔH = −x, then B→A has ΔH = +x.

    How to use Hess’s law

    1. Write the target equation.
    2. Reverse given equations if needed (reverse the sign of ΔH).
    3. Multiply equations to match coefficients (multiply ΔH by the same factor).
    4. Add equations and cancel species appearing on both sides; add their enthalpy changes.

    Thermochemical equations can therefore be added, subtracted, or multiplied like algebraic equations.

    Illustrations and applications

    • Carbon to CO₂: direct C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH about −393.8 kJ; via C(s) + ½O₂(g) → CO(g), −110.62 kJ, then CO(g) + ½O₂(g) → CO₂(g), −283.49 kJ. The staged total is about −394.11 kJ, close to the direct value (experimental data differ slightly).
    • NaOH formation: the chapter’s two routes give about −653.14 and −655.23 kJ for the same overall reaction; their 2.09 kJ difference is attributed to experimental error.
    • Indirect heats of formation: useful when compounds such as methane, CO, or benzene cannot conveniently be made directly from their elements.
    • Heat of transition: compare heats of combustion of two allotropes; the common product cancels.
    • Heat of other reactions: combine known reactions to find an otherwise difficult-to-measure heat, e.g. NO₂ dimerization.

    9. Measuring reaction heat: calorimetry

    Calorimetry is experimental measurement of heat or enthalpy change using a calorimeter. Apparatus is chosen for the reaction being studied.

    Water calorimeter

    Used for reactions in solution. A thermally insulated water bath contains a known mass of water; a thermometer and stirrer pass through its lid. Reactants are mixed in a reaction chamber immersed in the bath. If W grams of water rises by t °C:

    Qwater = W × 1 cal g−1 °C−1 × t   (in calories)
    In SI: Qwater = mwatercwaterΔT
    Heat of reaction = − heat gained by water (with signed heat convention).

    Bomb calorimeter

    Used mainly for combustion of organic compounds. A weighed sample burns in oxygen in a sealed steel bomb, typically at about 2.03 MPa (20 atm). The bomb is immersed in a known amount of water in an insulated copper vessel with a stirrer and sensitive thermometer (the text specifies readings to 0.01°); ignition is electrical. The temperature rise and calorimeter heat capacity give the heat released. Because the bomb is rigid, the measurement gives Qv = ΔE.

    Second Law of Thermodynamics

    Spontaneity, entropy, heat engines, free energy, phase equilibria, and chemical potential

    1. Spontaneous processes

    Spontaneous (natural) process: a process that proceeds by itself under the stated conditions, without continuous outside assistance. The reverse direction is nonspontaneous unless work is supplied. The tendency to occur naturally is spontaneity.
    Everyday examples and what they show
    Spontaneous directionReverse direction
    A person falls from a cliff.Climbing back up requires work.
    Heat flows from a hot metal object to a cold one.Moving heat from cold to hot needs work, as in a refrigerator.
    Gas spreads into an evacuated vessel.Compressing it back into the original vessel requires work.
    • A spontaneous change is directional; reversing it requires work.
    • Spontaneity does not predict speed: a spontaneous reaction may be fast or very slow.
    • An unstable, nonequilibrium system tends to change until equilibrium is reached.
    • An undisturbed equilibrium system has no net spontaneous change.
    • A fall in enthalpy often favours spontaneity, but it is not the only factor: melting ice and evaporation can be spontaneous even though they absorb heat.

    The missing factor is entropy, associated with energy dispersal, molecular disorder, and the number of accessible arrangements.

    2. Entropy and the second law

    Entropy, S: a thermodynamic state function that measures the dispersal, randomness, or disorder of a system’s molecular arrangements. Its change is ΔS = Sfinal − Sinitial.

    Ice has a relatively ordered crystal arrangement; liquid water is less ordered; water vapour has much greater molecular freedom. Melting and evaporation increase the water’s entropy. A shuffled deck has many possible random arrangements, while only one arrangement is perfectly ordered—disordered states are overwhelmingly more probable.

    Useful tendency: an increase in entropy often favours a process. But the exact criterion is for the system plus surroundings, not necessarily the system alone.
    Second law: every spontaneous, irreversible change increases the total entropy of the universe (system + surroundings).
    ΔSuniv = ΔSsys + ΔSsurr
    Spontaneous irreversible change: ΔSuniv > 0
    Reversible change / equilibrium limit: ΔSuniv = 0

    The extraction’s phrase “increase in total energy” is a transcription error: the second law concerns entropy; energy is conserved by the first law. The entropy of the universe tends toward a maximum. This law explains why heat naturally flows hot→cold, why engines cannot be 100% efficient, and why many natural and biochemical processes have a preferred direction.

    3. Third law, entropy units, and standard entropy

    Third law: the entropy of a perfect pure crystal is zero at absolute zero: S = 0 at T = 0 K.

    As temperature falls, molecular motion and the number of accessible arrangements usually decrease. Gas → liquid → solid generally lowers entropy; at 0 K, a perfect crystal is in its unique, perfectly ordered state.

    Numerical definition and units

    For a reversible change, Clausius defined entropy change by the heat transferred reversibly divided by absolute temperature:

    dS = δQrev/T
    At fixed T:   ΔS = Qrev/T

    Heat absorbed gives a positive entropy change; heat released gives a negative one. Units are J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (SI) or cal mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. One entropy unit, 1 eu = 1 cal mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ = 4.184 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

    Absolute and standard entropy

    The third law allows absolute entropies to be calculated from 0 K upward. Standard entropy, S°, is the absolute entropy at the standard state; the chapter uses 298 K and 1 atm. Unlike standard enthalpy of formation, elemental standard entropies at 298 K are generally not zero; they are positive.

    4. Cycles, heat engines, and the Carnot cycle

    Cyclic process and heat engine

    A cycle is a sequence of processes that returns a system to its starting state. Since E is a state function, ΔE = 0 over a cycle, so net heat equals net work output.

    ΔEcycle = 0;   Qnet = Wby,net

    A heat engine takes heat Qh from a hot reservoir, converts some to work, and rejects the rest Qc to a cooler sink. It runs cyclically. Its efficiency is:

    η = Wby/Qh = (Qh − |Qc|)/Qh

    No cyclic heat engine can turn all heat from one reservoir into work; some heat must be rejected to a cooler sink.

    Carnot cycle: four reversible operations

    1. Isothermal reversible expansion at hot temperature Th (T₂): heat Qh enters; the ideal gas expands.
    2. Adiabatic reversible expansion: Q = 0; temperature falls from Th to Tc (T₁).
    3. Isothermal reversible compression at Tc: heat is rejected to the cold reservoir.
    4. Adiabatic reversible compression: Q = 0; temperature rises back to Th, returning the system to its starting state.

    Work and efficiency of a reversible Carnot engine

    For one mole, the hot isothermal expansion absorbs Qh = RTh ln(V₂/V₁). The cold isothermal compression rejects heat. The adiabatic legs exchange no heat; their work contributions cancel in the cycle’s net-work expression. The adiabatic relation makes V₂/V₁ = V₃/V₄.

    Wnet = Qh − |Qc| = R(Th − Tc) ln(V₂/V₁)
    ηCarnot = Wnet/Qh = (Th − Tc)/Th = 1 − Tc/Th

    Temperatures must be in kelvin. A larger hot–cold temperature difference gives greater theoretical efficiency. Carnot theorem: every reversible engine working between the same two temperatures has the same efficiency, independent of the working substance.

    Equivalent statements of the second law

    • Kelvin–Planck: no cyclic engine can take heat from a single hot reservoir and convert it completely into work; some heat must go to a cooler reservoir.
    • Clausius: heat cannot be transferred in a cycle from a colder body to a hotter body without work being supplied.

    5. Entropy from the Carnot cycle

    For a reversible Carnot cycle, the signed heat transfers at the two temperatures obey:

    Qh/Th + Qc/Tc = 0
    For any reversible cycle:   ∮ δQrev/T = 0

    Any reversible cycle can be built from many small Carnot cycles; internal paths cancel. Therefore ∫δQrev/T between two equilibrium states is independent of path and defines a state function, entropy:

    ΔS = S₂ − S₁ = ∫12 δQrev/T;   dS = δQrev/T

    6. Entropy change in irreversible processes and ideal gases

    For an irreversible cycle, the Clausius inequality applies: ∮δQ/T < 0. In nature, spontaneous changes are irreversible and increase the entropy of the universe. An isolated system undergoing a spontaneous change has ΔS > 0.

    Derive ideal-gas entropy change

    For n moles of an ideal gas, δQrev = dE + P dV, dE = nCVdT, and P = nRT/V. Dividing by T gives:

    dS = nCV(dT/T) + nR(dV/V)
    ΔS = nCV ln(T₂/T₁) + nR ln(V₂/V₁)

    Using the ideal-gas equation to express volume in terms of P and T:

    ΔS = nCP ln(T₂/T₁) + nR ln(P₁/P₂)
    = nCP ln(T₂/T₁) − nR ln(P₂/P₁)
    Useful ideal-gas entropy formulas
    PathEntropy change
    Isothermal (T₁ = T₂)ΔS = nR ln(V₂/V₁) = nR ln(P₁/P₂)
    Isobaric (P₁ = P₂)ΔS = nCP ln(T₂/T₁)
    Isochoric (V₁ = V₂)ΔS = nCV ln(T₂/T₁)

    Free expansion into a vacuum: an important entropy example

    For an ideal gas expanding freely into a vacuum inside a perfectly insulated box, Q = 0 and W = 0, so ΔE = 0 and the gas temperature is unchanged. Yet entropy increases: calculate it using an imagined reversible isothermal path between the same states:

    ΔSgas = nR ln(V₂/V₁) > 0 when V₂ > V₁

    The actual irreversible free expansion has no heat transfer, so do not use Qactual/T to find its entropy change. Entropy is a state function; use a reversible path. The isolated surroundings do not change, so ΔSuniv = ΔSgas > 0.

    7. Entropy change accompanying phase changes

    A reversible phase change occurs at constant temperature while the two phases are in equilibrium. For one mole:

    Melting:   ΔSf = ΔHf/Tf
    Vaporization:   ΔSv = ΔHv/Tb
    Allotropic transition:   ΔSt = ΔHt/Tt
    Sublimation:   ΔSsub = ΔHsub/Tsub

    Examples: vaporizing one mole of water at 373 K with latent heat 40,402.62 J mol⁻¹ gives ΔS ≈ 108.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Melting one mole of ice at 273 K, using 334.94 J g⁻¹ × 18 g mol⁻¹, gives about 22.09 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

    8. Gibbs free energy and Helmholtz work function

    Gibbs free energy: G = H − TS = E + PV − TS. It is a state function and an extensive property.
    Helmholtz work function: A = E − TS. It is also a state function.

    For a change at constant temperature:

    ΔG = ΔH − TΔS
    ΔA = ΔE − TΔS
    Since H = E + PV:   ΔG = ΔA + PΔV (for constant P and T)
    • At constant T and P, −ΔG is the maximum useful non-PV work obtainable from a reversible process.
    • At constant T and V, −ΔA is the maximum work obtainable from a reversible process.

    For a simple compressible system with PV work only:

    dG = VdP − SdT
    At constant P: (∂G/∂T)P = −S
    At constant T: (∂G/∂P)T = V

    For n moles of an ideal gas in an isothermal change:

    ΔG = nRT ln(P₂/P₁) = nRT ln(V₁/V₂)

    An ideal-gas expansion has P₂ < P₁, so ΔG is negative.

    9. Gibbs–Helmholtz equations

    These equations connect free-energy changes with enthalpy or internal-energy changes, and can be used when free energies at different temperatures are known.

    At constant pressure:   ΔG = ΔH + T(∂ΔG/∂T)P
    Equivalent standard form:   (∂(ΔG/T)/∂T)P = −ΔH/T²
    At constant volume:   ΔA = ΔE + T(∂ΔA/∂T)V
    Equivalent standard form:   (∂(ΔA/T)/∂T)V = −ΔE/T²

    These identities follow from G = H − TS, A = E − TS, and the temperature dependence of G and A. Keep the sign and the held-constant condition clear.

    10. Conditions for spontaneity and equilibrium

    The general entropy criterion can be written for a simple system with PV work as:

    dSsys + dSsurr ≥ 0
    TdS − dE − P dV ≥ 0

    11. Clapeyron equation

    The Clapeyron equation describes equilibrium between two phases of a pure substance. During reversible liquid–vapour change at temperature T and equilibrium pressure p, the work is pΔV; combining this with the first law and the Gibbs–Helmholtz relation gives:

    dp/dT = ΔH/[T(V₂ − V₁)] = ΔH/(TΔV)

    Here ΔH is the molar enthalpy of transition and ΔV is the molar volume change from phase 1 to phase 2. The equation applies generally to phase boundaries (fusion, vaporization, sublimation, and other transitions).

    12. Clausius–Clapeyron equation

    For liquid–vapour equilibrium, the liquid volume is usually negligible compared with the vapour volume. If the vapour behaves ideally, V ≈ RT/p. The Clapeyron equation becomes:

    d(ln p)/dT = ΔHv/(RT²)
    If ΔHv is approximately constant:
    ln(p₂/p₁) = (ΔHv/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)
    log₁₀(p₂/p₁) = [ΔHv/(2.303R)](1/T₁ − 1/T₂)

    This approximate relation connects vapour pressure and temperature. A similar treatment can be used for solid–liquid equilibrium when the relevant volume and enthalpy data are known.

    14. Van’t Hoff reaction isotherm

    The chapter contents list the Van’t Hoff isotherm. It relates free-energy change to reaction composition at a fixed temperature. For a reaction quotient Q:

    ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q
    At equilibrium, ΔG = 0 and Q = K:
    ΔG° = −RT ln K = −2.303RT log₁₀K

    Here K is the equilibrium constant in the chosen standard-state convention. A related temperature relation is the van’t Hoff equation:

    d(ln K)/dT = ΔH°/(RT²)
    If ΔH° is nearly constant: ln(K₂/K₁) = −(ΔH°/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁)

    15. Fugacity, activity, and chemical potential

    These terms appear in the chapter contents and extend the free-energy treatment to real gases and non-ideal solutions.

    Fugacity, f

    An effective pressure that corrects for non-ideal gas behaviour. For an ideal gas, f ≈ P. Chemical potential of a gas can be written:

    μ = μ° + RT ln(f/f°)

    Activity, a

    An effective concentration or “escaping tendency” that corrects for non-ideality. For a gas, a = f/f°; in a solution, activity is often written as an activity coefficient times a concentration or mole-fraction scale.

    μ = μ° + RT ln a
    Chemical potential, μᵢ: the partial molar Gibbs free energy of component i—the change in G when a small amount of i is added at fixed T, P, and amounts of other components.
    μᵢ = (∂G/∂nᵢ)T,P,nⱼ
    dG = Σμᵢdnᵢ   (at fixed T and P)

    At equilibrium, each component has the same chemical potential in phases that can exchange it. A chemical reaction or transport tends to proceed in the direction that lowers the appropriate total Gibbs energy.

    Supplied written-question bank: concise model answers

    The answers below map directly to the 17 thermodynamics prompts supplied with the chapter.
    1. Hess’s law and Lavoisier–Laplace law, with an example

    Hess: total heat change is independent of route, because enthalpy is a state function. Example: C → CO₂ directly has approximately the same ΔH as C → CO followed by CO → CO₂; the two step enthalpies add. Lavoisier–Laplace: forward and reverse reactions have equal heat magnitudes and opposite signs: if A→B has ΔH = −x, B→A has +x.

    2. Define an adiabatic system and show TVγ−1 = constant

    An adiabatic system exchanges no heat, Q = 0. For a reversible ideal-gas change, dE = −P dV and dE = nCVdT. With P = nRT/V, integrate CVdT/T = −R dV/V. Using γ = CP/CV and CP−CV=R gives TVγ−1 = constant; also PVγ = constant.

    3. Show ΔH = heat at constant pressure; derive CV

    H = E + PV, so at constant P, ΔH = ΔE + PΔV. From the first law ΔE = Qp − PΔV for PV work only; hence ΔH = Qp. At constant volume dV = 0, so δQv = dE and CV = (∂E/∂T)V per mole.

    4. Heat of transition, heat of sublimation, and entropy/spontaneity

    Heat of transition: enthalpy change when one mole of an element changes between allotropes. Heat of sublimation: enthalpy change when one mole of solid changes directly into gas. An entropy increase often favours spontaneity because more disordered arrangements are more probable; the exact criterion is ΔSuniv > 0, not necessarily ΔSsystem > 0 by itself.

    5. Define thermodynamics; state its scope and limitations

    Thermodynamics studies heat and other energy transfers during physical and chemical changes. It predicts feasibility and equilibrium extent under stated conditions and supports laws such as the Phase Rule. It is a macroscopic treatment, does not resolve individual molecular structure, and does not predict reaction rates.

    6. Define CV and CP; show CP − CV = R

    CV = (∂E/∂T)V; CP = (∂H/∂T)P, per mole. For an ideal gas H = E + RT, so differentiating gives CP = CV + R. CP exceeds CV because constant-pressure heating also supplies expansion work.

    7. State the second law and its significance

    For a spontaneous irreversible change, ΔSuniv = ΔSsys + ΔSsurr > 0; at reversible equilibrium it is zero. It predicts natural direction, explains why heat flows hot→cold, and proves that no cyclic heat engine can be 100% efficient.

    8. Define molar heat capacity and isothermal process; prove reversible work is maximum

    Molar heat capacity is heat needed to raise one mole by 1 K under a specified process. Isothermal means constant temperature. In a reversible expansion, Pext is kept infinitesimally below Pgas, the largest pressure opposing expansion without causing compression; integrating this pressure over volume gives the greatest possible work output.

    9. Compare reversible/irreversible, adiabatic/isothermal, open/closed
    • Reversible: infinitely slow, equilibrium at every stage, ideal and maximum work; irreversible: finite-rate, real, equilibrium mainly at endpoints, less work.
    • Adiabatic: Q = 0; temperature may change. Isothermal: T constant; heat may flow to keep T constant.
    • Open system: exchanges matter and energy. Closed system: exchanges energy but not matter.
    10. Show reversible isothermal work exceeds irreversible work

    Wby,rev = ∫V₁V₂PgasdV. An irreversible expansion against final pressure P₂ gives Wby,irr = P₂(V₂−V₁). Since during expansion Pgas is above P₂ until the endpoint, the reversible area under the P–V curve is larger: Wby,rev > Wby,irr.

    11. Relate ΔH to ΔE and derive heat capacities

    At constant pressure ΔH = ΔE + PΔV; for ideal-gas reactions ΔH = ΔE + ΔngRT. Heat capacities are CV = (∂E/∂T)V and CP = (∂H/∂T)P; for an ideal gas CP − CV = R.

    12. Define an adiabatic system

    A system whose boundary allows no heat transfer into or out of it: Q = 0. It may still exchange work; only an isolated system exchanges neither matter nor energy.

    13. Define enthalpy; calculate reversible expansion work, 1 mol, 25°C, 10→20 L

    Enthalpy is H = E + PV. At 25°C ≈ 298 K, Wby = nRT ln(V₂/V₁) = 1×8.314×298×ln2 ≈ +1.717 kJ = +410.5 cal. The signed work on the system is −1.717 kJ.

    14. Water vaporization at 100°C: find Q, W, and ΔE

    For 1 mol, Q = +9720 cal = +40.67 kJ. Steam volume is approximately RT/P ≈ 30.62 L; liquid volume is 0.018 L. Thus Wby = PΔV ≈ +3.10 kJ (work on system ≈ −3.10 kJ). Therefore ΔE = Q − Wby ≈ +37.57 kJ (about 8980 cal).

    15. Insulated free expansion into a vacuum: entropy? Define heat of transition

    Q = 0 and W = 0, so ΔE = 0; for an ideal gas, T stays constant. Entropy increases: ΔS = nR ln(V₂/V₁) > 0, calculated along a reversible path between the same states. Heat of transition is the enthalpy change when one mole of an element changes allotrope.

    16. Distinguish reversible and irreversible processes

    A reversible process is an ideal infinitely slow sequence of equilibrium states and can be reversed by an infinitesimal change; it gives maximum expansion work. An irreversible process is real, proceeds through finite gradients or rates, is not in equilibrium throughout, and gives less work in expansion.

    17. One mole gas, reversible isothermal expansion 5 atm→1 atm at 25°C

    Wby = nRT ln(P₁/P₂) = 1×8.314×298×ln5 ≈ +3.988 kJ. With the “work on system” sign convention, won ≈ −3.988 kJ.
