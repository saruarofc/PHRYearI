Introduction, Empirical Laws, Scope & Limitations

Definition of Thermodynamics Thermodynamics is the study of the flow of heat or any other form of energy into or out of a system as it undergoes a physical or chemical transformation.

To evaluate energy flow, we measure changes in macroscopic properties—such as temperature, pressure, volume, and concentration—from the initial state to the final state. These changes provide information about energy, heat, and work.

The Three Empirical Laws

  • Thermodynamics is based on three broad generalizations derived from well-established experimental results: the First, Second, and Third Laws of Thermodynamics.
  • These laws have stood the test of time and are independent of any theory of atomic or molecular structure.

Scope of Thermodynamics

  1. Derives Physical Chemistry Laws: Most important laws—including van’t Hoff law of lowering of vapor pressure, Phase Rule, and Distribution Law—can be derived from thermodynamic laws.
  2. Predicts Feasibility: Tells whether a particular physical or chemical change can occur under a given set of conditions of temperature, pressure, and concentration.
  3. Predicts Extent of Reaction: Helps predict how far a physical or chemical change can proceed until equilibrium conditions are established.

Limitations of Thermodynamics

  1. Macroscopic Only (Bulk Matter): Applicable only to macroscopic systems consisting of matter in bulk, and not to microscopic systems of individual atoms or molecules. It ignores the internal structure of atoms and molecules.
  2. Ignores Time Factor (Kinetics): Does not bother about the time factor—it tells nothing about the rate (speed) of a physical change or chemical reaction. It is concerned only with the initial and final states of the system.

Thermodynamic Terms, Systems & Properties

System, Boundary, and Surroundings

Core Definitions
  • System: That part of the universe which is under thermodynamic study.
  • Surroundings: The rest of the universe outside the system.
  • Boundary: The real or imaginary surface separating the system from the surroundings.

Experimental Examples

  • Beaker of Water (Fig. 7.2): 0.200 kg of water in a beaker constitutes the system. The beaker and air in contact are the surroundings.
  • Gas in a Cylinder: 1 mole of oxygen confined in a cylinder fitted with a piston is the system. The cylinder, piston, and all outside objects form the surroundings (clearly defined boundary).
Figures 7.1 & 7.2: Thermodynamic system, boundary, and water in a beaker.

Homogeneous and Heterogeneous Systems

Homogeneous System

When a system is uniform throughout, it is called a Homogeneous System. It consists of one phase only.

Examples: A pure single solid, liquid, or gas; mixtures of gases; a true solution of a solid in a liquid.

Heterogeneous System

A system which consists of two or more phases (not uniform throughout).

Examples: Ice in contact with water; ice in contact with vapor (ice, water, and vapor constitute separate phases).

Definition of Phase A phase is defined as a homogeneous, physically distinct, and mechanically separable portion of a system.

Types of Thermodynamic Systems

Depending on the nature of the boundary: if closed/sealed, no matter can pass through; if insulated, no energy (heat) can pass through.

Figure 7.3: Three types of thermodynamic systems based on boundary exchange.

Intensive and Extensive Properties

Macroscopic or bulk properties of a system are divided into two classes:

Intensive Properties (Non-Additive)

A property which does not depend on the quantity of matter present in the system.

Example: If a glass of water is at 293 K with NaCl concentration 0.1 mol/L (0.0001 m3), any single drop of water from that glass has the exact same temperature (293 K) and concentration.

Extensive Properties (Additive)

A property that depends on the quantity of matter present in the system. Extensive properties are additive.

Example: If we double the mass of water in a glass, its volume, number of moles, and internal energy are all doubled.

State of a System, Equilibrium & Thermodynamic Processes

State of a System & State Variables

State of a System A thermodynamic system is said to be in a certain state when all its properties are fixed. The fundamental properties that determine the state of a system—Pressure (P), Temperature (T), Volume (V), Mass, and Composition—are called State Variables, State Functions, or Thermodynamic Parameters.
  • Equation of State: For a pure gas (100% fixed composition), the remaining variables P, V, T are interrelated algebraically. For 1 mole of a pure ideal gas: P V = R T
  • Independent vs. Dependent State Variables: If P and T are specified, V is fixed automatically via PV = RT. Thus, P and T are independent state variables, while V is a dependent state variable.
  • Key Characteristic of a State Function: Change in a state variable depends only on the initial and final states, not on the path taken. (Example: Heating water from 273.15 K to 298.15 K always gives ΔT = 25 K regardless of how it is heated.)

Equilibrium and Non-Equilibrium States

Thermodynamic Equilibrium (Fig. 7.4a)

A system in which the state variables have constant values throughout the system. Example: A gas in a cylinder with a stationary frictionless piston has uniform P and V.

Non-Equilibrium State (Fig. 7.4b)

A system in which state variables have different values in different parts. Example: If the piston is pushed down very rapidly, gas near the piston is compressed and heated more than gas at the far end.

The Three Criteria for Thermodynamic Equilibrium Note: Thermodynamics is concerned only with equilibrium states.
  1. Thermal Equilibrium: The temperature of the system must be uniform and equal to the temperature of the surroundings.
  2. Mechanical Equilibrium: Mechanical properties must be uniform throughout the system (no mechanical work is done by one part of the system on another).
  3. Chemical Equilibrium: Chemical composition of the system must be uniform with no net chemical change. (In a heterogeneous system, state variables remain constant within each phase.)

Thermodynamic Processes

When a thermodynamic system changes from one state to another, the operation is called a Process.

Process Type Definition & Condition Mathematical Condition How it is Achieved / Example
1. Isothermal Temperature remains fixed dT = 0 Placing the system in a thermostat (constant temperature bath).
2. Adiabatic No heat flows into or out of the system dq = 0 Carried out in an insulated container (e.g., thermos bottle with high vacuum and highly polished surfaces).
3. Isobaric Takes place at constant pressure dp = 0 Heating of water to its boiling point and its vaporization at constant atmospheric pressure.
4. Isochoric Volume remains constant dV = 0 Heating a substance in a rigid, non-expanding chamber.
5. Cyclic Process System goes through a series of processes and returns to its initial state dE = 0, dH = 0 Closed loop on a P–V diagram (Fig. 7.5b).
Figure 7.5: (a) Four basic thermodynamic processes; (b) A cyclic process.

Reversible and Irreversible Processes

Reversible Process (Fig. 7.6a) A process that takes place infinitesimally slowly through an infinite series of infinitesimally small stages, such that its direction at any point can be reversed by an infinitesimal change in the state of the system (e.g., decreasing piston pressure from P to P – dP). The system remains in equilibrium at all stages.
Irreversible Process (Fig. 7.6b) A process that goes from the initial to the final state in a single rapid step (e.g., sudden drop in external pressure from P1 to P2) and cannot be carried out in the reverse order. The system is in equilibrium only in the beginning and at the end, not at intermediate points.

Differences Between Reversible and Irreversible Processes

# Reversible Process Irreversible Process
1 Takes place in an infinite number of infinitesimally small steps and takes infinite time to occur. Takes place in a single step in finite time.
2 It is imaginary (ideal) as it assumes a completely frictionless and weightless piston. It is real and can be performed actually.
3 System is in an equilibrium state at all stages of the operation. System is in equilibrium only at the initial and final stages of the operation.
4 All changes are reversed when the process is carried out in the reverse direction. After the process occurs, changes do not return to the initial state by themselves.
5 It is extremely slow. It proceeds at a measurable speed.
6 Work done by a reversible process is greater (maximum) than the corresponding irreversible process. Work done by an irreversible process is smaller than the corresponding reversible process.

Nature of Heat, Work & Isothermal Expansion of an Ideal Gas

Units of Work and Heat

Units of Mechanical Work (Force × Distance)

  • Erg (CGS unit): Work done when a resistance of 1 dyne is moved through 1 cm.
  • Joule (SI unit): 1 J = 107 ergs and 1 kJ = 1000 J
  • Work can also be expressed in liter-atmospheres (L·atm).

Units of Heat (q)

  • Calorie (cal): Quantity of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 g of water by 1°C in the vicinity of 15°C.
  • SI Unit of Heat is Joule (J):
    1 cal = 4.184 J  |  1 kcal = 4.184 kJ  |  1 J = 0.2390 cal

Sign Conventions of Heat (q) and Work (w)

Figure 7.7 & Summary of Sign Conventions: Energy entering the system is positive (+); energy leaving is negative (–).

Pressure–Volume (PV) Work

Consider a gas in a cylinder fitted with a frictionless piston of cross-sectional area A against an external pressure Pext. (Crucial Rule: Always use external pressure Pext, not internal gas pressure, to evaluate work!)

  • Since Pressure = Force / Area, the opposing force is: F = Pext × A
  • When the gas expands at constant pressure and pushes the piston through a distance l:
    Work = Force × Distance = F × l = Pext × A × l = Pext × ΔV
    where ΔV = A × l = (V2 – V1) is the increase in volume.
  • Since in expansion the system (gas) does work on the surroundings, it carries a negative sign:
    w = – Pext ΔV = – Pext (V2 – V1)
    (For compression, the piston moves down, V2 < V1, and the sign of work w is positive.)

Isothermal Reversible vs. Irreversible Expansion of an Ideal Gas

1. Isothermal Reversible Expansion Work (Fig. 7.9)

External pressure is reduced infinitesimally by dP at each step so Pext = Pgas – dP ≈ P. Work in one small step dV is dw = – P dV.

Integrating from V1 to V2 using P = nRT / V:

wrev = – ∫V1V2 P dV = – nRT ∫V1V2 dVV
wrev = – nRT lnV2V1 = – 2.303 nRT logV2V1

Since P1V1 = P2V2 at constant T:

wrev = – 2.303 nRT logP1P2
2. Isothermal Irreversible Expansion Work

The external pressure Pext is instantaneously dropped to the final pressure P2. The expansion takes place against constant pressure P2 throughout:

wirr = – P2 (V2 – V1)

Why Reversible Work is Maximum Work:

Work depends on Pext. The largest value Pext can have without causing compression is equal to Pgas at every stage—which is the condition for reversible expansion. Thus, |wrev| > |wirr|. Because work depends on the path taken between states A and B, mechanical work is a path function, not a state function.

Internal Energy (E) & First Law of Thermodynamics

Internal Energy (E or U)

Definition of Internal Energy The total of all the possible kinds of energy of a system (translational kinetic energy of molecules, rotational and vibrational energies, and kinetic and potential energy of nuclei and electrons within molecules) is called its Internal Energy (E or U).
  • State Function & Extensive Property: Internal energy depends only on the state of the system (independent of path) and is proportional to the mass of matter in the system.
    Example: Heating 1 mole of liquid water from 273 K to 373 K always gives ΔE = 7.5362 kJ regardless of whether energy is supplied by heating, mechanical work, or electrical means.
  • Absolute Value Not Needed: It is neither possible nor necessary to calculate the absolute value of E; thermodynamics only measures the change: ΔE = Ef – Ein = E2 – E1
  • Units: SI unit is Joule (J); non-SI unit is calorie (1 cal = 4.184 J).

First Law of Thermodynamics

Statements of the First Law (Law of Conservation of Energy)
  1. Main Statement: The total energy of an isolated system remains constant, though it may change from one form to another.
  2. Closed System Statement: The net energy change of a closed system is equal to the heat transferred to the system minus the work done by the system:
    ΔE = EB – EA = q – w    or    ΔE = q – P ΔV
  3. Alternative Definitions:
    • Whenever energy of a particular type disappears, an equivalent amount of another type must be produced.
    • Total energy of a system and surroundings remains constant (or conserved).
    • It is impossible to construct a perpetual motion machine that can produce work without spending energy on it.
Important Note on Textbook Formula Convention (ΔE = q – w) In the mathematical formula ΔE = q – w (illustrated in Fig. 7.11), the textbook defines w = P ΔV as the work done by the expanding gas (so expansion work subtracts from internal energy, while absorbed heat q adds to it). Be careful to note whether a question asks for the magnitude of work done by the gas (+w) or the signed value (–w).

Four Special Cases of the First Law (ΔE = q – w)

Case 1: Cyclic or Isothermal Ideal Gas Process

Since internal energy depends only on temperature for an ideal gas (or returns to initial state in a cycle):

ΔE = 0  ⇒  q = w

Case 2: Isochoric Process (Constant Volume)

Since there is no change in volume (ΔV = 0), no work of expansion is done (w = 0):

ΔE = qv

Case 3: Adiabatic Process (Insulated)

No heat is gained or lost by the system (q = 0):

ΔE = – w

(Decrease in internal energy equals work done by the system.)

Case 4: Isobaric Process (Constant Pressure)

Pressure P remains constant during volume change ΔV:

ΔE = qp – P ΔV

Enthalpy of a System (H) & Relation Between ΔH and ΔE

Definition of Enthalpy (H) The total heat content of a system at constant pressure is equivalent to the internal energy E plus the PV energy. This is called the Enthalpy (Greek: en = in; thalpos = heat) of the system:
H = E + P V
  • Enthalpy is a State Function: Because E, P, and V are all state functions, H is also a state function and is independent of path.
  • Derivation of ΔH = qp:
    • Change in enthalpy: ΔH = H2 – H1 = (E2 + P2V2) – (E1 + P1V1) = (E2 – E1) + (P2V2 – P1V1)
    • At constant pressure P: ΔH = ΔE + P (V2 – V1) = ΔE + P ΔV
    • From the First Law (ΔE = q – P ΔV), substituting gives: ΔH = (qp – P ΔV) + P ΔV = qp
    • Thus, ΔH equals the heat absorbed or evolved at constant pressure.
  • Sign Conventions & Units of ΔH:
    • If H2 > H1, then ΔH is positive (+ve) and the reaction is endothermic.
    • If H1 > H2, then ΔH is negative (–ve) and the reaction is exothermic.
    • Example: H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l) + 286.042 kJ means ΔH = – 286.042 kJ.
    • Units: kilocalories (kcal) or SI unit kilojoules (kJ).

Relation Between ΔH and ΔE for Gaseous Reactions

Derivation of ΔH = ΔE + Δn RT

Volumes of solids and liquids are negligibly small compared to gases. Suppose a reaction involves n1 moles of gases before reaction and n2 moles of gases after reaction at constant temperature T and pressure P:

  • For ideal gases: P V1 = n1 R T and P V2 = n2 R T
  • Subtracting: P (V2 – V1) = P ΔV = (n2 – n1) R T = Δn R T
  • Substituting P ΔV = Δn R T into ΔH = ΔE + P ΔV gives:
ΔH = ΔE + Δn R T

where Δn = (number of moles of gaseous products) – (number of moles of gaseous reactants). (Note: When Δn = 0, ΔH = ΔE.)

Molar Heat Capacities (Cv and Cp)

Definitions & Units

  • Heat Capacity of Unit Mass (Specific Heat, c): Heat absorbed by unit mass in raising the temperature by 1 degree (K or °C): c = qm (T2 – T1)
  • Molar Heat Capacity (C): The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 mole of the substance by 1 K. Since C varies with temperature, its true value is the ratio of small heat absorbed dq to small temperature rise dT: C = qT2 – T1    or    C = dqdT
  • Units: cal K–1 mol–1 or SI unit J K–1 mol–1.
  • Not a State Function: Because heat q is not a state function, heat capacity is also not a state function. Hence we must specify whether the process is at constant volume (Cv) or constant pressure (Cp).
1. Molar Heat Capacity at Constant Volume (Cv)

From the First Law: dq = dE + P dV. Dividing by dT:

dqdT = dEdT + P dVdT

At constant volume, dV = 0, so:

Cv = dqvdT = (∂E∂T)V

Definition: Rate of change of internal energy with temperature at constant volume.

2. Molar Heat Capacity at Constant Pressure (Cp)

Since H = E + PV, differentiating w.r.t. T at constant pressure P gives:

(∂H∂T)P = (∂E∂T)P + P (∂V∂T)P

Comparing with the First Law expression at constant pressure:

Cp = dqpdT = (∂H∂T)P

Definition: Rate of change of enthalpy with temperature at constant pressure.

Relation Between Cp and Cv for an Ideal Gas

Why Cp > Cv and Proof that Cp – Cv = R

Physical Reason: At constant volume, all absorbed heat goes into increasing internal energy (temperature). At constant pressure, part of the absorbed heat is used to increase internal energy and the remaining part is used in doing expansion work (P ΔV) against the surroundings. Hence more heat is needed at constant pressure to achieve the same 1 K rise, so Cp is always greater than Cv.

Mathematical Derivation:

  • For 1 mole of an ideal gas: H = E + P V = E + R T
  • Differentiating w.r.t. temperature T: dHdT = dEdT + R
  • Substituting Cp = dH/dT and Cv = dE/dT:
    Cp = Cv + R    or    Cp – Cv = R
    where R = 1.987 cal K–1 mol–1 = 8.314 J K–1 mol–1.

Calculation of ΔE and ΔH for n Moles of an Ideal Gas:

ΔE = n × Cv × (T2 – T1)     and     ΔH = n × Cp × (T2 – T1)

Joule–Thomson Effect (Joule–Kelvin Effect)

Definition of Joule–Thomson Effect The phenomenon of producing a lowering of temperature when a gas is made to expand adiabatically from a region of high pressure into a region of low pressure through a porous plug (or throttle) is known as the Joule–Thomson Effect (or Joule–Kelvin Effect).
Figure 7.12: Joule–Thomson experiment showing adiabatic expansion across a porous plug.

Joule–Thomson Experiment & Proof of Constant Enthalpy (ΔH = 0)

  • Experimental Setup (Fig. 7.12): A gas of volume V1 at high pressure P1 is forced slowly through a porous plug in an insulated tube by Piston A, expanding to volume V2 and low pressure P2 at Piston B.
    Observation: Most gases undergo cooling on expansion, except Hydrogen (H2) and Helium (He), which show warming at room temperature.
  • Thermodynamic Proof that ΔH = 0 (Isoenthalpic Process):
    • Work done on the gas at Piston A = P1V1; Work done by the gas at Piston B = P2V2.
    • Net work done by the gas: w = P2V2 – P1V1
    • Since the tube is thermally insulated (adiabatic), q = 0. By the First Law:
      ΔE = E2 – E1 = q – w = – w = – (P2V2 – P1V1) = P1V1 – P2V2
    • Rearranging: E2 + P2V2 = E1 + P1V1  ⇒  H2 = H1 or ΔH = 0. Thus, the Joule–Thomson expansion takes place at constant enthalpy.
Joule–Thomson Coefficient (μ) & Inversion Temp

The number of degrees temperature change produced per atmosphere drop in pressure under constant enthalpy conditions:

μ = (∂T∂P)H
  • If μ is positive (+): Gas cools on expansion (most gases at room temperature; used in liquefaction of gases).
  • If μ is negative (–): Gas warms on expansion (H2 and He at room temperature).
  • Inversion Temperature: The temperature at which the sign of μ changes (μ = 0). For H2, the inversion temperature is given as –193.15 K (or –80°C / 193 K). H2 and He must first be cooled below their inversion temperature (using liquid N2) to liquefy by JT expansion.
  • For an Ideal Gas: μ = 0 (no intermolecular forces).
Physical Explanation of the JT Effect

In the Joule–Thomson process, enthalpy is constant:

H = E + P V = constant
  • Most Gases (Cooling): As pressure drops, the product PV increases, which must be compensated by a decrease in internal energy E (work done against intermolecular attractions). Lower E causes a fall in temperature (T2 < T1).
  • H2 and He at Room Temp (Warming): PV decreases with lowering of pressure, causing an increase in internal energy E and a rise in temperature (T2 > T1). Below the inversion temperature, PV increases with pressure drop and cooling is produced.

Adiabatic Expansion of an Ideal Gas & Work Done

1. Derivation of P–V–T Relations for Adiabatic Reversible Expansion

In an adiabatic process, q = 0, so dE = – dw = – P dV. Because work is done at the expense of internal energy, E decreases and temperature falls.

Step-by-Step Derivation of TVγ–1 = k and PVγ = k
  1. For 1 mole of an ideal gas, dE = Cv dT and P = RT / V. Substituting into dE = – P dV:
    Cv dT = – P dV = – R TV dV   ⇒   Cv dTT + R dVV = 0
  2. Integrating between (T1, V1) and (T2, V2) with Cv constant:
    Cv lnT2T1 + R lnV2V1 = 0
  3. Since R = Cp – Cv and defining the heat capacity ratio γ = Cp / Cv, we have R / Cv = (Cp – Cv) / Cv = γ – 1:
    lnT2T1 + (γ – 1) lnV2V1 = 0   ⇒   T2T1 = (V1V2)γ–1   ⇒   T Vγ–1 = constant
  4. Pressure–Volume Relation: Using the ideal gas law T2 / T1 = (P2V2) / (P1V1):
    P2 V2P1 V1 = (V1V2)γ–1   ⇒   P1 V1γ = P2 V2γ = constant
  5. Temperature–Pressure Relation: Eliminating volume using V1 / V2 = (P2T1) / (P1T2):
    (T1T2)γ = (P1P2)γ–1

2. Comparison Between Isothermal and Adiabatic Expansions

Why the Adiabatic Curve is Steeper (Fig. 7.13)

  • Isothermal Expansion (PV = constant): Heat is absorbed from the surroundings to make up for the work done by the gas, so temperature remains constant.
  • Adiabatic Expansion (PVγ = constant): For a monoatomic ideal gas, γ = 1.67. Expansion takes place at the expense of internal energy, so temperature falls and pressure drops more rapidly for a given increase in volume. Thus, the adiabatic P–V curve is steeper than the isothermal curve.
Figure 7.13: Isothermal vs. adiabatic expansion curves.

3. Work Done in Adiabatic Reversible Expansion (4-Step Derivation)

Derivation of wmax in Adiabatic Reversible Expansion
  • Step 1 (Value of V dP from adiabatic equation): Differentiating P Vγ = k gives γ Vγ–1 P dV + Vγ dP = 0. Dividing by Vγ–1: V dP = – γ P dV   (1)
  • Step 2 (Value of V dP from ideal gas equation): Differentiating P V = R T (for 1 mole) gives P dV + V dP = R dT: V dP = R dT – P dV   (2)
  • Step 3 (Substitution): Equating (1) and (2): R dT – P dV = – γ P dV  ⇒  R dT = P (1 – γ) dV  ⇒  P dV = R dT1 – γ
  • Step 4 (Integration): Integrating from (T1, V1) to (T2, V2):
    wmax = ∫V1V2 P dV = R (T2 – T1)1 – γ  (for 1 mole)    or    wmax = n R (T2 – T1)1 – γ = – n Cv (T2 – T1)  (for n moles)

Sign Interpretation in Textbook Formula: Because 1 – γ is negative (since γ > 1): when T2 > T1, wmax is negative (work done on the gas); when T2 < T1, wmax is positive (work done by the gas).

Solved Problem 1: Pressure–Volume Work in Constant Pressure Expansion

Question: Calculate the pressure-volume work done when a system containing a gas expands from 0.001 m3 to 0.002 m3 against a constant external pressure of 1013250.00 Pa. Express the answer in calories and joules.

Solution:

  • Given: V1 = 0.001 m3, V2 = 0.002 m3, Pext = 1013250.00 Pa (N m–2)
  • Formula: w = – Pext (V2 – V1)
  • Substitution: w = – 1013250.00 Pa × (0.002 – 0.001) m3 = – 1013.25 J
  • In Calories: Since 1 cal = 4.184 J, w = – 1013.25 / 4.184 = – 242.17 cal
Solved Problem 2: Isothermal Reversible Expansion Work (Given Volumes)

Question: One mole of an ideal gas at 298 K is allowed to expand reversibly at constant temperature from a volume of 0.01 m3 to 0.02 m3. Calculate the work done by the gas in joules and calories.

Solution:

  • Given: n = 1 mol, T = 298 K, V1 = 0.01 m3, V2 = 0.02 m3, R = 8.314 J K–1 mol–1
  • Formula: w = – 2.303 n R T log(V2 / V1)
  • Substitution: w = – 2.303 × 1 × 8.314 × 298 × log(0.02 / 0.01) = – 5705.85 × 0.3010 = – 1717.46 J (Work done by gas = 1717.46 J)
  • In Calories: w = – 1717.46 / 4.184 = – 410.48 cal
  • Notes from text: (1) ln x = 2.303 log x; (2) Units of R determine units of w; (3) Temperature must be in Kelvin.
Solved Problem 3: Isothermal Reversible Expansion Work (Given Pressures)

Question: Find the work done when one mole of the gas is expanded reversibly and isothermally from 5 atm (506625.00 Pa) to 1 atm (101325.00 Pa) at 298 K.

Solution:

  • Given: n = 1 mol, T = 298 K, P1 = 506625.00 Pa, P2 = 101325.00 Pa, R = 8.314 J K–1 mol–1
  • Formula: w = – 2.303 n R T log(P1 / P2)
  • Substitution: w = – 2.303 × 1 × 8.314 × 298 × log(506625.00 / 101325.00) = – 5705.85 × log(5) = – 5705.85 × 0.69897 = – 3988.2 J
Solved Problem 4: Finding ΔE, q, and w in Isothermal Reversible Expansion

Question: Find ΔE, q, and w if 2 moles of hydrogen at 303975.00 Pa pressure expand isothermally at 323 K and reversibly to a pressure of 101325.00 Pa.

Solution:

  • Since the operation is isothermal and the gas is ideal: ΔE = 0.
  • From the First Law: ΔE = q – w = 0  ⇒  q = w.
  • For a reversible process: w = – 2.303 n R T log(P1 / P2)
  • w = – 2 × 8.314 J K–1 mol–1 × 323 K × 2.303 × log(303975.00 / 101325.00)
  • w = – 2 × 8.314 × 323 × 2.303 × log(3) = – 5903.387 J
  • Since q = w: q = – 5903.387 J
Solved Problem 5: Change in Internal Energy During Vaporisation of Water

Question: 0.001 kg of water at 373 K is converted into steam at the same temperature. The volume of water becomes 0.001671 m3 on boiling. Calculate the change in the internal energy of the system if the heat of vaporisation is 2260980 J/kg.

Solution:

  • Heat absorbed (q): q = mass × latent heat = 0.001 kg × 2260980 J/kg = 2260.98 J
  • Volume change (ΔV): Initial volume of 0.001 kg (1 g) liquid water is V1 = 1 cm3 = 0.000001 m3; Final volume of steam is V2 = 0.001671 m3.
  • Work done against 1 atm (101325 Pa): w = P (V2 – V1) = 101325 Pa × (0.001671 – 0.000001) m3 = 101325 × 0.001670 = 169.21 J
  • Internal Energy Change (ΔE): ΔE = q – w = 2260.98 J – 169.21 J = 2091.77 J
Solved Problem 6: First Law Calculation with Heat Absorption and Expansion

Question: A gas contained in a cylinder fitted with a frictionless piston expands against a constant external pressure of 101325.00 Pa from a volume of 0.005 m3 to a volume of 0.01 m3. In doing so it absorbs 400 J thermal energy from its surroundings. Determine ΔE for the process.

Solution:

  • Given: q = + 400 J, Pext = 101325.00 Pa, V1 = 0.005 m3, V2 = 0.01 m3
  • Work done by the gas (w): w = Pext (V2 – V1) = 101325.00 × (0.01 – 0.005) = 506.625 J
  • From First Law: ΔE = q – w = 400 J – 506.625 J = – 106.625 J
Solved Problem 7: Maximum Work, ΔE, and q for Hydrogen Expansion

Question: Calculate the maximum work done when pressure on 0.01 kg of hydrogen is reduced from 2026500.00 Pa to 101325.00 Pa at a constant temperature of 273 K. The gas behaves ideally. Will there be any change in internal energy? Also calculate ‘q’.

Solution:

  • Moles of H2 (n): n = 0.01 kg / 0.002 kg mol–1 = 10 g / 2 g mol–1 = 5 moles
  • Maximum Work (wmax): w = – 2.303 n R T log(P1 / P2) = – 2.303 × 5 × 8.314 × 273 × log(2026500.00 / 101325.00)
  • w = – 26133.91 × log(20) = – 26133.91 × 1.3010 = – 34000.9 J (Magnitude of work done = 34000.9 J)
  • Change in Internal Energy (ΔE): Since the process is isothermal and the gas is ideal, ΔE = 0.
  • Heat (q): Since ΔE = q – w = 0, q = w = 34000.9 J (or –34000.9 J with textbook sign convention).
Solved Problem 8: Calculating ΔH from ΔE for H2F2 Dissociation

Question: For the reaction H2F2(g) → H2(g) + F2(g), ΔE = – 59.4526 kJ/mol at 298 K. Calculate ΔH for the reaction.

Solution:

  • Formula: ΔH = ΔE + Δn R T
  • Moles of gas: n2 = 1 + 1 = 2; n1 = 1; so Δn = n2 – n1 = 2 – 1 = 1
  • Textbook Calculation: ΔH = – 59.4526 + (1 × 8.314 × 298) = – 59.4526 + 2477.572 = 2418.1194 (printed as – 2418.1194 kJ/mol in text).
  • Unit-Consistent Note (Converting R to kJ): Since 2477.572 J = 2.4776 kJ, in consistent kJ units: ΔH = – 59.4526 kJ + 2.4776 kJ = – 56.975 kJ/mol.
Solved Problem 9 (& Problem 16): Calculating ΔE and ΔH on Heating Oxygen

Question: Calculate the value of ΔE and ΔH on heating 0.064 kg of oxygen from 273 K to 373 K. Cv and Cp on an average are 20.93 J mol–1 K–1 and 29.31 J mol–1 K–1 (29.308 in Problem 16).

Solution:

  • Formulas: ΔE = n × Cv × (T2 – T1) and ΔH = n × Cp × (T2 – T1)
  • Using Molecular Mass of O2 = 0.032 kg/mol (as solved in Problem 16):
    • n = 0.064 kg / 0.032 kg mol–1 = 2 moles
    • ΔE = 2 × 20.93 × (373 – 273) = 4186 J

Solved Problem 10: Heat Required to Raise Temperature of Water

Question: Calculate the amount of heat necessary to raise 0.2135 kg of water from 298 K to 373 K. Molar heat capacity of water is 75.36 J mol–1 K–1.

Solution:

  • Formula: q = n × C × (T2 – T1)

Solved Problem 11: Finding ΔE and ΔH for an Ideal Gas State Change

Question: Three moles of an ideal gas (Cv = 20.93 J K–1 mol–1) at 1013250 Pa and 273 K are converted to 202650.00 Pa at 323 K. Find ΔE and ΔH for the change. (R = 8.314 J mol–1 K–1)

Solution:

  • Given: n = 3 mol, T1 = 273 K, T2 = 323 K, Cv = 20.93 J K–1 mol–1
  • 1. Calculate ΔE: ΔE = n × Cv × (T2 – T1) = 3 × 20.93 × (323 – 273) = 3 × 20.93 × 50 = 3139.5 J
  • 2. Calculate Cp and ΔH:
    • Cp = Cv + R = 20.93 + 8.314 = 29.244 J K–1 mol–1
    • ΔH = n × Cp × (T2 – T1) = 3 × 29.244 × (323 – 273) = 4386.6 J
Solved Problem 12: Work Done in Adiabatic Reversible Expansion

Question: Calculate w for the adiabatic reversible expansion of 2 moles of an ideal monoatomic gas (γ = 5/3) at 273.2 K and 2026500.00 Pa to a final pressure of 202650.00 Pa.

Solution:

  • Step 1 (Calculate final temperature T2):
    (T2T1)γ = (P2P1)γ–1  ⇒  (T2273.2)5/3 = (202650.002026500.00)5/3 – 1 = (220)2/3
    Solving gives: T2 = 273.2 × (0.1)2/5 = 108.8 K
  • Step 2 (Calculate maximum adiabatic work):
    wmax = n R (T2 – T1)1 – γ = 2 × 8.314 × (108.8 – 273.2)1 – 5/3 = 4100.8 J
  • Alternative Solution (via ΔE): Since Cv = (3/2) R = 1.5 × 8.314 = 12.471 J K–1 mol–1, ΔE = n Cv (T2 – T1) = 2 × 12.471 × (108.8 – 273.2) = – 4100.8 J, so work done by the gas is w = – ΔE = 4100.8 J.
Solved Problem 13: Combustion of Liquid Benzene (ΔH to ΔE)

Question: At 298 K for the combustion of 1 mole of liquid benzene, the heat of reaction at constant pressure is given by:
C6H6(l) + 7½ O2(g) → 6 CO2(g) + 3 H2O(l), ΔH = – 3269807.017 J. What would be the heat of reaction at constant volume (ΔE)?

Solution:

  • Formula: ΔH = ΔE + Δn R T  ⇒  ΔE = ΔH – Δn R T
  • Moles of gas change (Δn): Δn = nP – nR = 6 – 7.5 = – 1.5
  • Substitution: ΔE = – 3269807 – (–1.5) × (8.314 × 298) = – 3269807 + 3716.358 = – 3266090.642 J = – 3266.090 kJ

Solved Problem 18: Final Volume and Final Temperature of Compressed Ideal Gas

Question: 5 moles of an ideal gas are compressed to a final pressure P2 = 10132500.00 Pa where the final volume is V2 = 0.00186 m3. (a) Find the final volume from the compression relation and (b) Calculate the final temperature T2 of the gas.
