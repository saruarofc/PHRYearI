QB Q13 (I.C 2025) — Enthalpy & Isothermal Reversible Work

Definition: Enthalpy (\(H\)) is the total heat content of a system at constant pressure, defined as \(H = E + PV\).

Problem: \(n = 1\text{ mol}\), \(T = 25^\circ\text{C} = 298\text{ K}\), \(V_1 = 10\text{ L}\), \(V_2 = 20\text{ L}\).

\[W_{\text{rev}} = 2.303 \, nRT \log_{10}\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right)\]
  • In Joules (\(R = 8.314\text{ J K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}\)): \(W_{\text{rev}} = 2.303 \times 1 \times 8.314 \times 298 \times \log_{10}\left(\frac{20}{10}\right) = 5705.85 \times 0.30103 = \mathbf{1717.6\text{ J}}\) (or \(-1717.6\text{ J}\) in IUPAC sign convention since work is done by the gas).
  • In Calories (\(R = 1.987\text{ cal K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}\), or \(1\text{ cal} = 4.184\text{ J}\)): \(W_{\text{rev}} = \frac{1717.6}{4.184} = \mathbf{410.5\text{ cal}}\) (using \(R = 1.987\): \(2.303 \times 1 \times 1.987 \times 298 \times 0.30103 = \mathbf{410.5\text{ cal}}\)).
QB Q14 (I.C 2024) — Vaporization of Water: Q, W, and \(\Delta E\)

Given: \(n = 1\text{ mol H}_2\text{O}(l) \to \text{H}_2\text{O}(g)\) at \(T = 100^\circ\text{C} = 373\text{ K}\), \(P = 1\text{ atm}\), \(Q = +9720\text{ cal/mol}\), \(V_{\text{liq}} = 0.018\text{ L/mol}\).

  • Heat absorbed: \(Q = \mathbf{+9720\text{ cal/mol}}\).
  • Volume of 1 mol steam (ideal gas): \(V_{\text{gas}} = \frac{nRT}{P} = \frac{1 \times 0.08206 \times 373}{1} = 30.61\text{ L/mol}\).
  • Change in volume: \(\Delta V = V_{\text{gas}} - V_{\text{liq}} = 30.61 - 0.018 = 30.59\text{ L/mol}\).
  • Work done by gas: \(W = P\Delta V = 1\text{ atm} \times 30.59\text{ L} = 30.59\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\).
    Since \(1\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm} = \frac{1.987}{0.08206} = 24.214\text{ cal}\): \(W = 30.59 \times 24.214 = \mathbf{740.7\text{ cal/mol}}\) (work done by the system; \(-740.7\text{ cal/mol}\) in IUPAC convention).
  • Internal Energy change (\(\Delta E\)): Using First Law (\(\Delta E = Q - W_{\text{by system}}\)): \(\Delta E = 9720 - 740.7 = \mathbf{8979.3\text{ cal/mol}}\).
QB Q15 (I.C 2024) — Free Expansion of an Ideal Gas in an Insulated Box

Answer: The entropy of the gas increases (\(\Delta S > 0\)).

Justification:

  1. In free expansion into a vacuum inside an insulated box, \(q = 0\) and \(P_{\text{ext}} = 0 \implies w = 0\), so \(\Delta E = 0\). For an ideal gas, \(\Delta T = 0\) (temperature stays constant).
  2. Although \(q = 0\), this process is spontaneous and irreversible, so we cannot set \(\Delta S = q_{\text{irrev}}/T = 0\).
  3. Because entropy \(S\) is a state function, we calculate \(\Delta S\) along a reversible isothermal path connecting the same initial \((V_1, T)\) and final \((V_2, T)\) states: \[\Delta S = nR \ln\left(\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right) > 0 \quad (\text{since } V_2 > V_1)\] Physically, the molecules now occupy a larger volume, increasing spatial randomness/disorder.

Heat of Transition: The enthalpy change (\(\Delta H\)) when one mole of an element changes from one allotropic form to another (e.g., \(\text{C}_{\text{diamond}} \to \text{C}_{\text{amorphous}}\), \(\Delta H = +13.82\text{ kJ}\)).

QB Q17 (P.F 2025) — Reversible Isothermal Expansion from 5 atm to 1 atm

Given: \(n = 1\text{ mol}\), \(P_1 = 5\text{ atm}\), \(P_2 = 1\text{ atm}\), \(T = 25^\circ\text{C} = 298\text{ K}\).

\[W_{\text{rev}} = 2.303 \, nRT \log_{10}\left(\frac{P_1}{P_2}\right)\] \[W_{\text{rev}} = 2.303 \times 1 \times 8.314 \times 298 \times \log_{10}\left(\frac{5}{1}\right) = 5705.85 \times 0.69897 = \mathbf{3988.2\text{ J}}\]

(Magnitude of work done by the gas \(= 3988.2\text{ J}\) or \(953.2\text{ cal}\); in IUPAC convention \(w = -3988.2\text{ J}\).)

First Law of Thermodynamics — Basic Concepts

Energy flow, thermodynamic systems, state functions, reversible work, internal energy, enthalpy, heat capacities, Joule–Thomson effect, and adiabatic expansion.

7.1 Definition, Empirical Laws, Scope & Limitations QB Q5

Definition — Thermodynamics The study of the flow of heat or any other form of energy into or out of a system as it undergoes a physical or chemical transformation is called Thermodynamics.

Thermodynamics is built on three empirical laws (First, Second, and Third Laws), derived directly from experimental observations and independent of atomic/molecular theories (plus the Zeroth Law defining thermal equilibrium).

Scope (Importance)
  1. Derives key laws: van't Hoff law of vapor pressure lowering, Phase Rule, Distribution Law, etc.
  2. Predicts feasibility: Tells whether a physical or chemical change can occur under given \(T, P,\) and concentration.
  3. Predicts extent: Helps determine how far a reaction proceeds before reaching equilibrium.
Limitations
  1. Macroscopic only: Applies to bulk matter, not individual atoms/molecules; ignores internal atomic/molecular structure.
  2. No time factor: Tells nothing about reaction rate/speed. Concerned only with initial and final states.

7.2 Thermodynamic Terms, Types of Systems & Properties QB Q2a, Q9c, Q12

  • System: Part of the universe under thermodynamic study (e.g., water in a beaker, gas in a cylinder).
  • Surroundings: Rest of the universe outside the system.
  • Boundary: Real or imaginary surface separating the system from its surroundings.
  • Homogeneous System: Uniform throughout; consists of a single phase (a homogeneous, physically distinct, mechanically separable portion). Examples: pure gas, gas mixture, true salt solution.
  • Heterogeneous System: Not uniform throughout; consists of two or more phases. Examples: ice + water, ice + water vapor.

7.3 State of a System, Equilibrium & Thermodynamic Processes QB Q8, Q9b

  • State of a System: Fixed when all macroscopic properties (\(P, T, V\), mass, composition) are fixed. These are state variables / state functions—their changes depend only on initial and final states, never the path taken.
  • Equation of State: Algebraic link between state variables, e.g., \(PV = nRT\) (with \(P, T\) as independent and \(V\) as dependent state variables).
  • Path Functions: Quantities like heat (\(q\)) and work (\(w\)) that depend on the path/method of change, not just endpoints.
  • Equilibrium vs Non-Equilibrium: In equilibrium, state variables are uniform and constant throughout the system. Thermodynamics deals strictly with equilibrium states.
    • 3 Criteria for Equilibrium: (1) Thermal equilibrium (uniform \(T = T_{\text{surr}}\)), (2) Mechanical equilibrium (no unbalanced forces/work within system), (3) Chemical equilibrium (uniform composition, no net reaction).

Types of Thermodynamic Processes

Differentiate: Isothermal vs Adiabatic Process (QB Q9b)
  • Temperature: Constant (\(dT = 0\)) in isothermal; changes (\(dT \neq 0\)) in adiabatic.
  • Heat exchange: Heat flows freely (\(q \neq 0\)) in isothermal; strictly no heat flows (\(q = 0\)) in adiabatic.
  • Internal energy (ideal gas): \(\Delta E = 0\) in isothermal; \(\Delta E = -w \neq 0\) in adiabatic.
  • PV Law & Curve: Follows \(PV = \text{const}\) in isothermal vs steeper \(PV^\gamma = \text{const}\) in adiabatic.

7.4 Reversible vs Irreversible Processes QB Q9a, Q16

  • Reversible Process: Proceeds infinitesimally slowly through an infinite series of equilibrium states; direction can be reversed at any point by an infinitesimal change (\(dP\)) in external conditions.
  • Irreversible Process: Proceeds rapidly in a single step (or finite steps) and cannot be reversed spontaneously; system is in equilibrium only at initial and final states.
Property Reversible Process Irreversible Process
Steps & TimeInfinite infinitesimally small steps; takes infinite time.Single or finite steps; occurs in finite time.
RealismIdeal / imaginary (assumes frictionless, weightless piston).Real; actually occurs in nature.
EquilibriumIn equilibrium at all intermediate stages.In equilibrium only at initial and final states.
ReversibilityAll changes can be completely reversed.Does not return to initial state by itself.

7.5 Nature of Heat, PV Work & Maximum Work Proof QB Q8, Q10, Q13, Q17

Important Note on Sign Conventions
  • Heat (\(q\)): Absorbed by system \(= +q\); released by system \(= -q\).
  • Work (\(w\)): The textbook writes First Law as \(\Delta E = q - w\) (where \(w = +P_{\text{ext}}\Delta V\) is work done by the system), whereas modern IUPAC writes \(\Delta E = q + w\) (where \(w = -P_{\text{ext}}\Delta V\) is negative when work is done by the system, and positive when done on the system). In both conventions, \(\Delta E\) is identical!

Pressure–Volume (PV) Work

Mechanical work is force \(\times\) distance. When a gas expands against external pressure \(P_{\text{ext}}\), moving a piston of area \(A\) by distance \(l\) (\(\Delta V = A \cdot l\)):

Key Work Formulas
  • Irreversible Isothermal Expansion (against constant \(P_{\text{ext}} = P_2\)): \[|W_{\text{irrev}}| = P_{\text{ext}}(V_2 - V_1) = P_2(V_2 - V_1)\]
  • Reversible Isothermal Expansion of \(n\) mol Ideal Gas (\(P_{\text{ext}} \approx P_{\text{gas}} = \frac{nRT}{V}\)): \[|W_{\text{rev}}| = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} P\, dV = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} \frac{nRT}{V}\, dV = nRT \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = 2.303\,nRT\log_{10}\frac{V_2}{V_1} = 2.303\,nRT\log_{10}\frac{P_1}{P_2}\] (For work done by system under IUPAC sign: \(w_{\text{rev}} = -2.303\,nRT\log_{10}(V_2/V_1)\).)

Proof: Work of Expansion is Maximum in a Reversible Process (QB Q8, Q10)

  1. Expansion work done by a gas depends directly on the opposing external pressure: \(dw = P_{\text{ext}}\, dV\). The larger the \(P_{\text{ext}}\), the greater the work done by the gas.
  2. However, \(P_{\text{ext}}\) cannot exceed \(P_{\text{gas}}\), otherwise compression would occur instead of expansion.
  3. Therefore, the maximum possible value of \(P_{\text{ext}}\) during expansion is \(P_{\text{ext}} = P_{\text{gas}} - dP \approx P_{\text{gas}}\), which is precisely the condition for a reversible expansion.
  4. Graphically (Fig. 7.2), the area under the reversible \(P\)–\(V\) curve \(\int P\,dV\) is strictly larger than the rectangular area \(P_2(V_2 - V_1)\) of a single-step irreversible expansion. Hence \(W_{\text{rev}} > W_{\text{irrev}}\).
Figure 7.2 — Shaded area under the curve proves \(W_{\text{rev}} > W_{\text{irrev}}\) between the same states.
Solved Example: Work Done at Constant Pressure & Reversible Pressure Change

Ex 1 (Constant Pressure): Gas expands from \(0.001\text{ m}^3\) to \(0.002\text{ m}^3\) against \(P_{\text{ext}} = 1013250\text{ Pa}\) (\(10\text{ atm}\)).
\(|W| = P_{\text{ext}}\Delta V = 1013250 \times (0.002 - 0.001) = \mathbf{1013.25\text{ J}} = \mathbf{242.17\text{ cal}}\).

Ex 2 (Reversible, 2 mol \(\text{H}_2\) at \(323\text{ K}\) from \(3\text{ atm}\) to \(1\text{ atm}\)):
\(\Delta E = 0\) (isothermal ideal gas), so \(q = W_{\text{by gas}} = 2.303 \times 2 \times 8.314 \times 323 \times \log_{10}(3/1) = \mathbf{5903.4\text{ J}}\).

7.6 Internal Energy & First Law of Thermodynamics

Internal Energy (\(E\) or \(U\)) The total of all possible kinds of energy within a system (translational, rotational, vibrational kinetic energy, plus nuclear & electronic kinetic/potential energies). It is an extensive property and a state function (\(\Delta E = E_f - E_i\)). SI unit: Joule (J); \(1\text{ cal} = 4.184\text{ J}\).

Statements of the First Law (Law of Conservation of Energy)

  1. The total energy of an isolated system (or universe) remains constant, though it may change from one form to another.
  2. Whenever energy of one kind disappears, an equivalent amount of another kind must be produced.
  3. It is impossible to construct a perpetual motion machine of the first kind (producing work without spending energy).
Mathematical Form & 4 Special Cases \[\Delta E = q - w \quad (\text{where } w = P_{\text{ext}}\Delta V \text{ is work done by system}) \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \Delta E = q + w_{\text{on sys}}\]
  • Case 1 (Cyclic / Isothermal Ideal Gas): \(\Delta E = 0 \implies q = w\).
  • Case 2 (Isochoric, \(dV = 0\)): \(w = 0 \implies \Delta E = q_v\).
  • Case 3 (Adiabatic, \(q = 0\)): \(\Delta E = -w_{\text{by sys}}\) (expansion cools the gas by lowering internal energy).
  • Case 4 (Isobaric, \(P = \text{const}\)): \(\Delta E = q_p - P\Delta V\).

7.7 Enthalpy (\(H\)), Proof of \(\Delta H = q_p\), and \(\Delta H\) vs \(\Delta E\) QB Q3, Q11, Q13

Enthalpy (\(H\)) Total heat content of a system at constant pressure: \(H = E + PV\). Since \(E, P, V\) are state functions, \(H\) is also a state function and an extensive property.

Proof: Change in Enthalpy equals Heat Absorbed/Released at Constant Pressure (QB Q3)

  1. Let a system change from state 1 \((H_1, E_1, V_1)\) to state 2 \((H_2, E_2, V_2)\) at constant pressure \(P\): \[H_1 = E_1 + PV_1, \qquad H_2 = E_2 + PV_2\]
  2. Subtracting: \[\Delta H = H_2 - H_1 = (E_2 - E_1) + P(V_2 - V_1) = \Delta E + P\Delta V\]
  3. From the First Law at constant pressure (considering only \(PV\) work): \(\Delta E = q_p - P\Delta V\), so \(q_p = \Delta E + P\Delta V\).
  4. Comparing the two expressions: \[\Delta H = q_p\] Thus, the enthalpy change equals the heat absorbed or evolved at constant pressure. (Endothermic: \(\Delta H > 0\); Exothermic: \(\Delta H < 0\).)

Relation Between \(\Delta H\) and \(\Delta E\) (QB Q11)

For chemical reactions involving ideal gases at constant \(T\) and \(P\), solids and liquids have negligible volume compared to gases. Let \(n_1\) = moles of gaseous reactants and \(n_2\) = moles of gaseous products, with \(\Delta n_g = n_2 - n_1\):

\[PV_1 = n_1 RT, \quad PV_2 = n_2 RT \implies P\Delta V = P(V_2 - V_1) = (n_2 - n_1)RT = \Delta n_g RT\]
Enthalpy–Internal Energy Relation \[\Delta H = \Delta E + \Delta n_g RT\]

Note: If \(\Delta n_g = 0\) (e.g., \(\text{H}_2(g) + \text{I}_2(g) \rightleftharpoons 2\text{HI}(g)\)), then \(\Delta H = \Delta E\).

Solved Example: Calculating \(\Delta H\) from \(\Delta E\)

For \(\text{C}_6\text{H}_6(l) + 7.5\,\text{O}_2(g) \to 6\text{CO}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)\) at \(298\text{ K}\), \(\Delta H = -3269.81\text{ kJ}\). Find \(\Delta E\).
\(\Delta n_g = 6 - 7.5 = -1.5\). Using \(R = 8.314 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kJ K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}\):
\(\Delta E = \Delta H - \Delta n_g RT = -3269.81 - (-1.5)(8.314 \times 10^{-3})(298) = -3269.81 + 3.72 = \mathbf{-3266.09\text{ kJ}}\).

7.8 Molar Heat Capacities & Proof of \(C_p - C_v = R\) QB Q3, Q6, Q11

Molar Heat Capacity (\(C\)) The amount of heat required to raise the temperature of 1 mole of a substance by \(1\text{ K}\) (or \(1^\circ\text{C}\)): \(C = \frac{dq}{dT}\) (units: \(\text{J K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}\) or \(\text{cal K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}\)). Because \(q\) is a path function, \(C\) must be specified at constant volume (\(C_v\)) or constant pressure (\(C_p\)).

Mathematical Derivation of \(C_v\) and \(C_p\) (QB Q3, Q11)

From the First Law for 1 mole (assuming only \(PV\) work): \(dq = dE + PdV\). Dividing by \(dT\):

\[\frac{dq}{dT} = \frac{dE}{dT} + P\frac{dV}{dT}\]
  • At Constant Volume (\(dV = 0\)): \[C_v = \left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial T}\right)_V \implies dE = C_v\,dT \quad (\text{1 mol}), \qquad \Delta E = nC_v\Delta T\] Definition: Rate of change of internal energy with temperature at constant volume.
  • At Constant Pressure (\(dP = 0\)): Since \(H = E + PV\), differentiating w.r.t. \(T\) at constant \(P\) gives \(\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\right)_P = \left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial T}\right)_P + P\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P = \left(\frac{dq}{dT}\right)_P\): \[C_p = \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\right)_P \implies dH = C_p\,dT \quad (\text{1 mol}), \qquad \Delta H = nC_p\Delta T\] Definition: Rate of change of enthalpy with temperature at constant pressure.

Proof: \(C_p - C_v = R\) for 1 Mole of an Ideal Gas (QB Q6)

  1. By definition, \(C_p = \frac{dH}{dT}\) and \(C_v = \frac{dE}{dT}\) (for an ideal gas, \(E\) depends only on \(T\)).
  2. Since \(H = E + PV\) and for 1 mole of an ideal gas \(PV = RT\): \[H = E + RT\]
  3. Differentiating both sides with respect to \(T\): \[\frac{dH}{dT} = \frac{dE}{dT} + R \implies C_p = C_v + R \implies \mathbf{C_p - C_v = R}\]

Why is \(C_p > C_v\)? At constant volume, all absorbed heat goes into raising internal energy (and temperature). At constant pressure, part of the absorbed heat is spent doing external expansion work (\(P\Delta V\)), so more heat is required to achieve the same \(1\text{ K}\) temperature rise.

7.9 Joule–Thomson Effect

Joule–Thomson (Joule–Kelvin) Effect The phenomenon of temperature change (usually cooling) when a compressed real gas expands adiabatically through a porous plug from a region of high pressure to a region of low pressure.

Proof of Constant Enthalpy (Isenthalpic): Net work done by gas \(= P_2 V_2 - P_1 V_1\). Since expansion is adiabatic (\(q = 0\)), \(\Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = -(P_2 V_2 - P_1 V_1) \implies E_2 + P_2 V_2 = E_1 + P_1 V_1 \implies \mathbf{H_2 = H_1}\) (\(\Delta H = 0\)).

  • Joule–Thomson Coefficient: \(\mu_{\text{JT}} = \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_H\).
    • \(\mu_{\text{JT}} > 0\): Gas cools on expansion (most gases at room temperature).
    • \(\mu_{\text{JT}} < 0\): Gas warms on expansion (e.g., \(\text{H}_2\) and \(\text{He}\) at room temp).
    • \(\mu_{\text{JT}} = 0\): For an ideal gas, or at the Inversion Temperature (\(T_i\)) where the sign reverses. (\(\text{H}_2\) must be pre-cooled below its inversion temp before liquefaction.)

7.10 Adiabatic Expansion of an Ideal Gas & Work Done QB Q2b

Proof: \(TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{Constant}\) in Adiabatic Expansion (QB Q2b)

  1. In an adiabatic process, \(dq = 0\). By the First Law for 1 mole of an ideal gas undergoing reversible expansion: \[dE = -dw = -P\,dV\]
  2. Since \(dE = C_v\,dT\) and \(P = \frac{RT}{V}\): \[C_v\,dT = -\frac{RT}{V}\,dV \implies C_v\frac{dT}{T} + R\frac{dV}{V} = 0\]
  3. Integrating between \((T_1, V_1)\) and \((T_2, V_2)\) assuming constant \(C_v\): \[C_v \ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} + R \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = 0 \implies \ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} + \frac{R}{C_v}\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = 0\]
  4. Using \(R = C_p - C_v\) and \(\gamma = \frac{C_p}{C_v}\), we have \(\frac{R}{C_v} = \gamma - 1\): \[\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} + (\gamma - 1)\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = 0 \implies \ln\left(\frac{T_2 V_2^{\gamma - 1}}{T_1 V_1^{\gamma - 1}}\right) = 0\]
  5. Taking antilog: \[T_1 V_1^{\gamma - 1} = T_2 V_2^{\gamma - 1} \implies \mathbf{TV^{\gamma - 1} = \text{Constant}}\]
  6. Substituting \(T = \frac{PV}{R}\) also yields \(\mathbf{PV^\gamma = \text{Constant}}\) and \(\mathbf{T^\gamma P^{1-\gamma} = \text{Constant}}\).
Work Done in Adiabatic Reversible Expansion (\(n\) moles) \[|W_{\text{adiab}}| = -\Delta E = nC_v(T_1 - T_2) = \frac{nR(T_1 - T_2)}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{P_1 V_1 - P_2 V_2}{\gamma - 1}\]

During adiabatic expansion, work is done at the expense of internal energy, so \(T_2 < T_1\) (cooling occurs), making the adiabatic \(P\)–\(V\) curve steeper than the isothermal curve (\(\gamma = 1.67\) monoatomic, \(1.40\) diatomic).

Chapter 8

Thermochemistry

Heat changes in chemical reactions, Lavoisier–Laplace law, Kirchhoff's equations, standard enthalpies, phase transitions, Hess's law, bond energy, and calorimetry.

8.1 Thermochemistry, Lavoisier–Laplace Law & Reaction Enthalpy QB Q1b

Thermochemistry & Lavoisier–Laplace Law (1782)
  • Thermochemistry: Branch of physical chemistry dealing with thermal (heat) changes accompanying chemical reactions.
  • Lavoisier and Laplace's Law (QB Q1b): The quantity of heat evolved in a chemical reaction is equal to the quantity of heat absorbed when the reaction is reversed under identical conditions: \(\Delta H_{\text{forward}} = -\Delta H_{\text{reverse}}\).
  • Exothermic Reaction: Heat is evolved (\(H_p < H_r\)); \(\Delta H < 0\) (negative). Examples: combustion, neutralization, condensation, rusting.
  • Endothermic Reaction: Heat is absorbed (\(H_p > H_r\)); \(\Delta H > 0\) (positive). Examples: melting ice, evaporation, photosynthesis, dissolving \(\text{NH}_4\text{NO}_3\) in water (cold packs).
  • Relation between \(\Delta H\) and \(\Delta E\): \(\Delta H = \Delta E + \Delta n_g RT\). For reactions involving only solids/liquids, \(\Delta V \approx 0 \implies \Delta H \approx \Delta E\).
Figure 8.1 — Enthalpy diagrams for exothermic and endothermic reactions.

8.2 Thermochemical Equations & Kirchhoff's Equations

A complete thermochemical equation must: (a) be balanced, (b) specify \(\Delta H\) or \(\Delta E\), and (c) state physical states \((s), (l), (g), (aq)\). Standard state is \(298\text{ K}\) and \(1\text{ atm}\) (\(101325\text{ Pa}\)), denoted \(\Delta H^\circ\).

Variation of Heat of Reaction with Temperature (Kirchhoff's Equations)

Since \(\Delta H = H_{\text{products}} - H_{\text{reactants}}\), differentiating with respect to \(T\) at constant pressure gives:

\[\left(\frac{\partial \Delta H}{\partial T}\right)_P = \left(\frac{\partial H_p}{\partial T}\right)_P - \left(\frac{\partial H_r}{\partial T}\right)_P = (C_p)_{\text{products}} - (C_p)_{\text{reactants}} = \Delta C_p\]
Kirchhoff's Equations (Integrated Form for Constant \(\Delta C_p, \Delta C_v\)) \[\Delta H_2 - \Delta H_1 = \Delta C_p (T_2 - T_1) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \Delta E_2 - \Delta E_1 = \Delta C_v (T_2 - T_1)\]

If \(C_p\) depends on \(T\): \(\Delta H_2 - \Delta H_1 = \int_{T_1}^{T_2} \Delta C_p\,dT\).

8.3 Types of Heat (Enthalpy) of Reaction

Enthalpy Type Definition & Key Rule
Heat of Formation (\(\Delta H_f^\circ\)) Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a compound is formed from its constituent elements in their standard states (\(298\text{ K}, 1\text{ atm}\)). By convention, \(\Delta H_f^\circ\) of all free elements in standard states = 0.
\(\Delta H^\circ_{\text{rxn}} = \sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants})\).
Heat of Combustion (\(\Delta H_c\)) Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a substance is completely burnt in excess \(\text{O}_2\). Always negative (\(\Delta H_c < 0\)).
Applications: Finding \(\Delta H_f\), fuel efficiency/calorific value (heat per gram, e.g., \(\text{CH}_4\) produces more heat/g than \(\text{C}_2\text{H}_6\)), and deciding molecular structure.
Heat of Solution Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a solute dissolves in a specified solvent (or at infinite dilution where further dilution produces zero thermal effect).
Heat of Neutralization Enthalpy change when 1 gram equivalent of an acid is neutralized by 1 gram equivalent of a base in dilute solution.
• Strong acid + Strong base \(\approx \mathbf{-57.36\text{ kJ}}\) (\(\text{H}^+ + \text{OH}^- \to \text{H}_2\text{O}\)).
• Weak acid/base gives a smaller magnitude (e.g., \(-51.50\text{ kJ}\)) because part of the heat is absorbed as heat of dissociation/ionization of the weak electrolyte.

8.4 Phase Changes: Fusion, Vaporization, Sublimation & Transition QB Q4, Q15

  • Heat of Fusion (\(\Delta H_{\text{fus}}\)): Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a solid converts to liquid at its melting point (e.g., ice \(\to\) water at \(273\text{ K}\): \(+5.98\text{ kJ/mol}\)). Higher \(\Delta H_{\text{fus}}\) indicates stronger intermolecular forces.
  • Heat of Vaporization (\(\Delta H_{\text{vap}}\)): Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a liquid converts to vapor at its boiling point (e.g., water \(\to\) steam at \(373\text{ K}\): \(+40.65\text{ kJ/mol}\)).
  • Heat of Sublimation (\(\Delta H_{\text{sub}}\)) (QB Q4): Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a solid converts directly into the gaseous state at a temperature below its melting point (e.g., \(\text{I}_2(s) \to \text{I}_2(g)\): \(+62.47\text{ kJ/mol}\)). Note: \(\Delta H_{\text{sub}} = \Delta H_{\text{fus}} + \Delta H_{\text{vap}}\).
  • Heat of Transition (\(\Delta H_{\text{trans}}\)) (QB Q4, Q15): Enthalpy change when 1 mole of an element changes from one allotropic form to another (e.g., \(\text{C}_{\text{diamond}} \to \text{C}_{\text{amorphous}}, \Delta H = +13.82\text{ kJ}\); \(\text{S}_{\text{monoclinic}} \to \text{S}_{\text{rhombic}}, \Delta H = -0.067\text{ kJ}\); \(\text{P}_{\text{white}} \to \text{P}_{\text{red}}, \Delta H = -4.30\text{ kJ}\)).

8.5 Hess's Law of Constant Heat Summation QB Q1a

Statement — Hess's Law (1840) If a chemical change is made to take place in two or more different ways—whether in a single step or in several intermediate steps—the total amount of heat evolved or absorbed (at constant \(P\) or \(V\)) is the same, regardless of the path by which the change is brought about.

Thermodynamic Proof: \(\Delta H\) and \(\Delta E\) are state functions depending only on initial and final states. If going \(A \to Z\) in 1 step released \(Q_1\) and via \(A \to B \to C \to Z\) released \(Q_2 > Q_1\), reversing \(Z \to A\) directly would create net energy \((Q_2 - Q_1)\) per cycle in an isolated system—violating the First Law of Thermodynamics. Hence \(Q_1 = Q_2\).

Example: Combustion of Carbon to \(\text{CO}_2\)

  • Path 1 (Direct): \(\text{C}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \to \text{CO}_2(g)\), \(\Delta H_1 = \mathbf{-393.5\text{ kJ}}\).
  • Path 2 (Two-Step):
    1. \(\text{C}(s) + \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2(g) \to \text{CO}(g)\), \(\Delta H_a = -110.5\text{ kJ}\)
    2. \(\text{CO}(g) + \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2(g) \to \text{CO}_2(g)\), \(\Delta H_b = -283.0\text{ kJ}\)
    Total heat: \(\Delta H_a + \Delta H_b = -110.5 + (-283.0) = \mathbf{-393.5\text{ kJ}} = \Delta H_1\).
Figure 8.2 — Hess's Law cycle showing path independence of \(\Delta H\).

Applications of Hess's Law: Thermochemical equations can be added, subtracted, and multiplied algebraically to find: (1) heats of formation of compounds that cannot be synthesized directly (\(\text{CH}_4, \text{CO}, \text{C}_6\text{H}_6\)), (2) heats of transition between allotropes, and (3) heats of slow/intermediate reactions (e.g., dimerization of \(\text{NO}_2\)).

8.6 Bond Energy & Calorimetry

  • Bond Energy: Average energy required to break 1 mole of a specific bond type in the gaseous state. Depends on atomic size, electronegativity, and bond length.
  • Sign Rule: Breaking bonds absorbs heat (\(+\)); forming bonds releases heat (\(-\)). \[\Delta H_{\text{rxn}} = \sum \text{B.E.(bonds broken)} - \sum \text{B.E.(bonds formed)}\]
  • Calorimetry:
    • Water Calorimeter: Measures heat of reactions in solution at constant pressure (\(q_p = \Delta H\)).
    • Bomb Calorimeter: Sealed steel vessel with excess \(\text{O}_2\) immersed in water; measures heat of combustion at constant volume (\(q_v = \Delta E\)), convertible to \(\Delta H\) via \(\Delta H = \Delta E + \Delta n_g RT\).
Solved Example: Bond Energy & Hess's Law Calculation

1. Hess's Law — Heat of formation of \(\text{C}_3\text{H}_8(g)\):
Given \(\Delta H_c(\text{C}_3\text{H}_8) = -2220.2\text{ kJ}\), \(\Delta H_f(\text{CO}_2) = -393.5\text{ kJ}\), \(\Delta H_f(\text{H}_2\text{O}) = -285.8\text{ kJ}\):
\(\Delta H_f(\text{C}_3\text{H}_8) = [3(-393.5) + 4(-285.8)] - (-2220.2) = -2323.7 + 2220.2 = \mathbf{-103.5\text{ kJ/mol}}\).

2. Bond Energy — \(\text{H}_2(g) + \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2(g) \to \text{H}_2\text{O}(g)\):
Given \(\text{B.E.(H–H)} = 435.4\text{ kJ}\), \(\text{B.E.(O=O)} = 494.0\text{ kJ}\), \(\text{B.E.(O–H)} = 464.7\text{ kJ}\):
\(\Delta H = [435.4 + \frac{1}{2}(494.0)] - [2(464.7)] = 682.4 - 929.4 = \mathbf{-247.0\text{ kJ/mol}}\).

Second Law of Thermodynamics

Spontaneity, entropy, Third Law, Carnot cycle, Gibbs & Helmholtz free energies, Gibbs–Helmholtz, Clausius–Clapeyron, van't Hoff isotherm/isochore, fugacity, chemical potential, and Zeroth Law.

9.1 Spontaneous Processes & Entropy QB Q4

  • Spontaneous Process: Proceeds of its own accord without outside assistance (unidirectional; e.g., heat flowing hot \(\to\) cold, gas expanding into vacuum, ice melting on a warm day).
  • Non-spontaneous Process: Does not occur on its own; requires external work.
  • Criteria of Spontaneity: (1) Unidirectional, (2) time is not a factor, (3) unstable non-equilibrium systems change spontaneously until equilibrium, (4) equilibrium systems do not change spontaneously, (5) exothermic processes (\(\Delta H < 0\)) tend to be spontaneous, but endothermic processes like melting ice and evaporation are also spontaneous—proving a second driving factor: Entropy (\(S\)).
Entropy (\(S\)) A thermodynamic state function that measures the randomness or molecular disorder of a system (\(\Delta S = S_{\text{final}} - S_{\text{initial}}\)).
Order of entropy: \(\text{Solid} < \text{Liquid} \ll \text{Gas}\). Units: \(\text{J K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}\) or \(\text{cal K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}\) (entropy unit, eu).

Explain: "A process accompanied by an increase in entropy tends to be spontaneous" (QB Q4)

  1. Consider a molecular system changing from an ordered, low-entropy State \(A\) to a disordered, high-entropy State \(B\) (like tossing ordered playing cards into a random pile).
  2. By probability (the law of chance), there are vastly more microstates/arrangements corresponding to the disordered State \(B\) than to the ordered State \(A\).
  3. Therefore, State \(B\) is thermodynamically much more probable than State \(A\), so nature spontaneously evolves from lower probability/order (\(A\)) to higher probability/disorder (\(B\)), accompanied by \(\Delta S > 0\).

9.2 Second Law & Third Law of Thermodynamics QB Q7

Statements of the Second Law of Thermodynamics (QB Q7)
  1. Entropy Statement: Whenever a spontaneous (irreversible) process takes place, it is accompanied by an increase in the total entropy of the universe: \[\Delta S_{\text{univ}} = \Delta S_{\text{sys}} + \Delta S_{\text{surr}} > 0 \quad (\text{reversible: } \Delta S_{\text{univ}} = 0)\]
  2. Kelvin–Planck Statement: It is impossible to take heat from a hot reservoir and convert it completely into work in a cyclic process without rejecting part of the heat to a cooler reservoir (no heat engine can be 100% efficient).
  3. Clausius Statement: It is impossible for a cyclic process to transfer heat from a colder body to a hotter body without supplying external work.
Significance of the Second Law (QB Q7)
  • Predicts the direction and spontaneity of physical, chemical, and biochemical processes (which the First Law cannot).
  • Establishes the upper limit on the efficiency of heat engines and power plants (\(\eta < 1\)).
  • Defines the arrow of time and introduces key state functions: Entropy (\(S\)) and Gibbs Free Energy (\(G\)), linking thermodynamics to chemical equilibrium.
Third Law of Thermodynamics & Standard Entropy
  • Statement: At absolute zero (\(0\text{ K}\)), all molecular vibrations cease in perfect order, so the entropy of a pure perfect crystal is zero (\(S = 0\) at \(T = 0\text{ K}\)).
  • Standard (Absolute) Entropy (\(S^\circ\)): Entropy of 1 mole of a substance at \(298\text{ K}\) and \(1\text{ atm}\) (always positive for \(T > 0\)).
  • Standard Entropy Change: \(\Delta S^\circ_{\text{rxn}} = \sum S^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum S^\circ(\text{reactants})\).

Numerical Definition of Entropy (Clausius, 1850): For a reversible process at fixed temperature \(T\), \(\Delta S = \frac{q_{\text{rev}}}{T}\) (or infinitesimally, \(dS = \frac{dq_{\text{rev}}}{T}\)).

9.3 Heat Engines, Carnot Cycle & Derivation of Entropy

A heat engine operates in a cycle, absorbing heat \(q_2\) from a hot source (\(T_2\)), converting part into work \(w\), and rejecting \(q_1\) to a cold sink (\(T_1\)).

Carnot Cycle (1824): An ideal reversible cycle using 1 mol ideal gas through 4 stages:

  1. Isothermal Reversible Expansion (\(A \to B\) at \(T_2\)): \(V_1 \to V_2\); heat absorbed \(q_2 = w_1 = RT_2 \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}\).
  2. Adiabatic Reversible Expansion (\(B \to C\)): \(V_2 \to V_3\), \(T_2 \to T_1\); \(q = 0\), \(w_2 = C_v(T_2 - T_1)\).
  3. Isothermal Reversible Compression (\(C \to D\) at \(T_1\)): \(V_3 \to V_4\); heat rejected \(|q_1| = |w_3| = RT_1 \ln\frac{V_3}{V_4}\).
  4. Adiabatic Reversible Compression (\(D \to A\)): \(V_4 \to V_1\), \(T_1 \to T_2\); \(q = 0\), \(w_4 = -C_v(T_2 - T_1)\).
Figure 9.1 — Carnot Cycle \(P\)–\(V\) indicator diagram.
Carnot Efficiency & Entropy Derivation

Since \(w_2 + w_4 = 0\) and \(\frac{V_2}{V_1} = \frac{V_3}{V_4}\) (from adiabatic \(TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}\)):

\[\eta = \frac{w}{q_2} = \frac{q_2 - |q_1|}{q_2} = \frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_2} = 1 - \frac{T_1}{T_2}\]
  • Carnot Theorem: Every reversible engine operating between the same \(T_2\) and \(T_1\) has the exact same efficiency, independent of working substance.
  • Derivation of Entropy: From \(\frac{q_2}{T_2} + \frac{q_1}{T_1} = 0\) (with signed heats) and breaking any reversible cycle into infinite Carnot cycles: \[\oint \frac{dq_{\text{rev}}}{T} = 0 \implies dS = \frac{dq_{\text{rev}}}{T} \text{ is an exact differential (state function).}\]
  • Irreversible Cycle: Efficiency is lower, so \(\oint \frac{dq}{T} < 0\) and \(\Delta S_{\text{univ}} > 0\) ("Entropy of the universe tends to a maximum").

9.4 Entropy Changes for an Ideal Gas & Phase Transitions

Ideal Gas Entropy Formulas (\(n\) moles)
  • In terms of \(T\) and \(V\): \(\Delta S = nC_v \ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} + nR \ln\frac{V_2}{V_1} = 2.303\,n\left(C_v\log_{10}\frac{T_2}{T_1} + R\log_{10}\frac{V_2}{V_1}\right)\)
  • In terms of \(T\) and \(P\): \(\Delta S = nC_p \ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} - nR \ln\frac{P_2}{P_1}\)
  • Special Cases:
    • Isothermal (\(T_1 = T_2\)): \(\Delta S_T = 2.303\,nR\log_{10}\frac{V_2}{V_1} = 2.303\,nR\log_{10}\frac{P_1}{P_2}\)
    • Isobaric (\(P_1 = P_2\)): \(\Delta S_P = 2.303\,nC_p\log_{10}\frac{T_2}{T_1}\)
    • Isochoric (\(V_1 = V_2\)): \(\Delta S_V = 2.303\,nC_v\log_{10}\frac{T_2}{T_1}\)

Entropy Change Accompanying Phase Changes

\(\Delta S_{\text{fus}} = \frac{\Delta H_{\text{fus}}}{T_f}\), \(\quad \Delta S_{\text{vap}} = \frac{\Delta H_{\text{vap}}}{T_b}\), \(\quad \Delta S_{\text{trans}} = \frac{\Delta H_{\text{trans}}}{T_t}\).

9.5 Gibbs Free Energy (\(G\)) & Helmholtz Work Function (\(A\))

  • Gibbs Free Energy (\(G\)): \(G = H - TS\). At constant \(T, P\): \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\).
    Decrease in \(G\) (\(-\Delta G\)) equals the maximum useful non-expansion (net) work obtainable at constant \(T, P\): \(-\Delta G = w_{\text{max}} - P\Delta V\).
  • Helmholtz Work Function (\(A\)): \(A = E - TS\). At constant \(T\): \(\Delta A = \Delta E - T\Delta S\).
    Decrease in \(A\) (\(-\Delta A\)) equals the maximum total reversible work: \(-\Delta A = w_{\text{rev}}\). Note: \(\Delta G = \Delta A + \Delta(PV)\).
  • Variation of \(G\) with \(T\) and \(P\): Since \(dG = VdP - SdT\): \[\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\right)_P = -S, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial P}\right)_T = V\]
  • Isothermal Change in \(G\) (Ideal Gas, \(n\) moles): \[\Delta G = nRT\ln\frac{P_2}{P_1} = 2.303\,nRT\log_{10}\frac{P_2}{P_1} = 2.303\,nRT\log_{10}\frac{V_1}{V_2}\]

9.6 Gibbs–Helmholtz Equations & Spontaneity Criteria

Gibbs–Helmholtz Equations
  • In terms of \(G\) and \(H\) (constant \(P\), closed system): \[\Delta G = \Delta H + T\left[\frac{\partial(\Delta G)}{\partial T}\right]_P \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \left[\frac{\partial(\Delta G/T)}{\partial T}\right]_P = -\frac{\Delta H}{T^2}\]
  • In terms of \(A\) and \(E\) (constant \(V\)): \[\Delta A = \Delta E + T\left[\frac{\partial(\Delta A)}{\partial T}\right]_V \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \left[\frac{\partial(\Delta A/T)}{\partial T}\right]_V = -\frac{\Delta E}{T^2}\]

Importance: Used to calculate \(\Delta H\) or \(\Delta E\) from \(\Delta G\) or \(\Delta A\) measured at two temperatures.

Conditions for Spontaneity & Equilibrium

Constraints Spontaneous (Irreversible) Equilibrium (Reversible)
Constant \(E, V\)\(dS > 0\)\(dS = 0\)
Constant \(S, V\)\(dE < 0\)\(dE = 0\)
Constant \(S, P\)\(dH < 0\)\(dH = 0\)
Constant \(T, P\)\(dG < 0\) (or \(\Delta G < 0\))\(dG = 0\) (or \(\Delta G = 0\))

Temperature Rule for \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\): Always spontaneous if \(\Delta H < 0, \Delta S > 0\); never spontaneous if \(\Delta H > 0, \Delta S < 0\); spontaneous at low \(T\) if both negative; spontaneous at high \(T\) if both positive.

9.7 Clapeyron & Clausius–Clapeyron Equations

  • Clapeyron Equation (Phase Equilibrium): \[\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{\Delta H}{T(V_2 - V_1)}\] where \(\Delta H\) is latent heat of transition and \(V_1, V_2\) are molar volumes of the two phases.
  • Clausius–Clapeyron Equation (Liquid \(\rightleftharpoons\) Vapor or Solid \(\rightleftharpoons\) Vapor): Neglecting \(V_{\text{liq}} \ll V_{\text{vap}}\) and assuming ideal vapor (\(V_{\text{vap}} = RT/P\)): \[\frac{d \ln P}{dT} = \frac{\Delta H_v}{RT^2} \implies \log_{10}\left(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\right) = \frac{\Delta H_v}{2.303\,R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\right) = \frac{\Delta H_v}{2.303\,R}\left(\frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1 T_2}\right)\]
  • 5 Applications: (1) Calculate latent heat of vaporization/fusion, (2) boiling/freezing point at another pressure, (3) vapor pressure at another temperature, (4) molal boiling-point elevation constant \(K_b = \frac{RT_b^2}{1000\,l_v}\), (5) molal freezing-point depression constant \(K_f = \frac{RT_f^2}{1000\,l_f}\) (where \(l_v, l_f\) are latent heats per gram).

9.8 van't Hoff Isotherm & van't Hoff Isochore

van't Hoff Equations for Chemical Equilibrium
  • van't Hoff Isotherm (derived via equilibrium box): \[-\Delta G = RT \ln K_p - RT \ln Q_p \quad \text{and at standard conditions (1 atm):} \quad -\Delta G^\circ = RT \ln K_p = 2.303\,RT\log_{10} K_p\] (\(K_p > 1 \implies \Delta G^\circ < 0\); \(K_p < 1 \implies \Delta G^\circ > 0\); \(K_p = 1 \implies \Delta G^\circ = 0\).)
  • van't Hoff Isochore (combining isotherm with Gibbs–Helmholtz): \[\frac{d(\ln K_p)}{dT} = \frac{\Delta H^\circ}{RT^2} \implies \log_{10}\left(\frac{K_{p2}}{K_{p1}}\right) = \frac{\Delta H^\circ}{2.303\,R}\left(\frac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1 T_2}\right)\]

9.9 Fugacity, Activity & Chemical Potential

  • Fugacity (\(f\)): Introduced by G.N. Lewis for real gases; represents the "escaping tendency" of a substance from a phase: \[G = RT\ln f + B, \qquad G - G^\circ = RT\ln\left(\frac{f}{f^\circ}\right)\] For an ideal gas, \(f = P\); for real gases, \(f \to P\) as \(P \to 0\).
  • Activity (\(a\)) & Activity Coefficient (\(\gamma\)): \(a = \frac{f}{f^\circ}\) (at standard state \(a = 1\)); \(\gamma = \frac{f}{P}\) measures deviation from ideality.
  • Partial Molar Property (\(\bar{X}_i\)): \(\bar{X}_i = \left(\frac{\partial X}{\partial n_i}\right)_{T,P,n_{j\neq i}}\) (contribution of 1 mole of component \(i\) to extensive property \(X\)).
  • Chemical Potential (\(\mu_i\)): Partial molar free energy: \[\mu_i = \bar{G}_i = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial n_i}\right)_{T,P,n_{j\neq i}}\] Matter flows spontaneously from higher \(\mu\) to lower \(\mu\); at equilibrium, \(\mu_i\) is uniform across all phases.
  • Gibbs–Duhem Equation (constant \(T, P\)): \[\sum n_i\,d\mu_i = 0 \quad (\text{from } G = \sum n_i\mu_i \text{ and } dG_{T,P} = \sum \mu_i\,dn_i)\]
  • Variation of \(\mu_i\) with \(T\) and \(P\): \[\left(\frac{\partial \mu_i}{\partial T}\right)_{P,n} = -\bar{S}_i, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial \mu_i}{\partial P}\right)_{T,n} = \bar{V}_i\]

9.10 Time's Arrow & Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics

  • Time's Arrow: Because entropy of macroscopic systems increases spontaneously in irreversible processes, the Second Law defines the forward direction of time.
  • Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics (R.H. Fowler): If two thermodynamic systems are separately in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are also in thermal equilibrium with each other (forms the basis of temperature & thermometry).
